\chapter{Hologram Generation with Spatial Light Modulators}
\addtocontents{toc}{\vskip-6pt\par\noindent\protect\textcolor{gray75}{\protect\rule{\textwidth}{0.5pt}}\par}
\label{chap:HologramGenerationwithSpatialLightModulators}
This chapter presents the numerical methods used to compute phase holograms, focusing on three key algorithms: Gerchberg--Saxton, mixed-region amplitude freedom and conjugate gradient minimisation. We evaluate their performance in generating high-fidelity optical potentials suitable for trapping atoms in reconfigurable arrays.

\section{Introduction}
Spatial light modulators (SLMs) have become indispensable tools in modern cold atom experiments to generate tailored optical potentials. In particular, liquid crystal SLMs offer dynamic, programmable phase modulation. By imprinting a spatially varying phase pattern---referred to as a kinoform---onto an incident laser beam, these modulators can shape the resulting diffraction pattern in the far field. This approach offers a high degree of flexibility and reconfigurability in shaping optical potentials, making it particularly well-suited for experiments involving microtrap arrays for single atoms~\cite{Bergamini2004,nogrette2014, Barredo2018,Schymik2020}, atom transport~\cite{Kim2016,Kim2019}, the study of superfluidity in ultracold atoms~\cite{Bruce2011a}, manipulation of Bose--Einstein condensates~\cite{Boyer2006} and time-varying potentials for evaporative cooling~\cite{Bruce2011b}.

In this chapter, we discuss and test numerical methods for computing kinoforms that produce regular arrays of optical microtraps. The underlying problem is a constrained phase retrieval task: given a known intensity profile in both the input (SLM) and output (target) planes, the goal is to find a phase pattern that transforms one into the other under Fourier propagation. We begin by reviewing a number of iterative Fourier transform algorithms and a gradient descent-based optimisation approach. We then proceed to benchmark their performance in generating optical potentials suitable for single-atom trapping.

\section{Algorithms for hologram generation}
As outlined in \autoref{setupoverview}, we employ a reflective liquid crystal SLM with an active area of $\SI{7.68}{\milli\meter}\times\SI{7.68}{\milli\meter}$ ($\num{512}\times\num{512}$ pixels) to imprint spatially varying phase profiles onto the incident beam. The objective is to engineer a far-field diffraction pattern that closely matches a prescribed target intensity distribution in the trapping plane. Although analytic kinoform solutions can be found for a limited class of target profiles~\cite{Matsumoto2008,Moulder2012}, the general problem of hologram generation is non-trivial and typically requires numerical techniques. The difficulty arises from the incomplete specification of the complex light field in both the input (SLM) and output (target) planes. In the target plane, the intensity is constrained by design, but the phase remains unconstrained. Conversely, in the SLM plane, the incident beam typically has a known Gaussian amplitude profile, while the phase is the degree of freedom to be optimised.

This formulation naturally leads to iterative Fourier transform algorithms (IFTAs), which include the Gerchberg--Saxton (GS) algorithm~\cite{GS} and its numerous refinements, such as weighted GS (GSW)~\cite{DiLeonardo2007}, mixed-region amplitude freedom (MRAF)~\cite{Pasienski2008}, offset-MRAF (OMRAF)~\cite{Gaunt2012}, and softness-OMRAF (SOMRAF)~\cite{somraf}. Alternative approaches include the Floyd--Steinberg error diffusion dithering algorithm~\cite{floyd} and optimisation-based methods such as conjugate gradient minimisation~\cite{Harte2014}. In the following sections, we explain the underlying principles and assess the performance of four representative methods (GS, GSW, MRAF and conjugate gradient minimisation) in generating arrays of high-fidelity optical traps.

\subsection{Gerchberg--Saxton}
The Gerchberg--Saxton algorithm is a foundational iterative method in phase retrieval and hologram generation, originally introduced in 1972~\cite{GS}. Unlike gradient-based approaches, the GS algorithm is not a minimisation technique. Instead, it relies on constraining the amplitude of the electric field in each plane while allowing the phase to evolve iteratively. The algorithm starts by defining the complex electric field in the input plane for the first iteration, defined over a grid of $N$ pixels:
\begin{equation}
\label{initialein}
E_{\mathrm{in}}^{(1)} = A_{\mathrm{in}}^{(0)} \exp{i \phi_{\mathrm{in}}^{(0)}}\,,
\end{equation}
where $A_{\mathrm{in}}^{(0)}$ is the fixed amplitude profile of the incident laser beam (typically Gaussian) and $\phi_{\mathrm{in}}^{(0)}$ is an initial guess for the phase. This field is then propagated to the output plane via a discrete Fourier transform:
\begin{align}
\label{forwardfourier}
\begin{split}
E_{\mathrm{out}}^{(1)}[x,y] &= \mathcal{F}\left\{ E_{\mathrm{in}}^{(1)} [p,q]\right\} \\&= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{p,q} E_\mathrm{in}^{(1)}[p,q] \exp\Bigl\{-2\pi i (px + qy) /N \Bigr\} \\&= A_{\mathrm{out}}^{(1)} \exp\Bigl\{i \phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{(1)} \Bigr\} \,,
\end{split}
\end{align}
where $[p,q]$ and $[x,y]$ represent the pixel indices in the input and output planes, respectively. The resulting amplitude in the output plane is written as $A_{\mathrm{out}}^{(1)}$, while the phase is denoted by $\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{(1)}$, where the superscripts refer to the iteration number.

At this stage, the computed output amplitude generally does not match the desired target amplitude $T[x,y]$. Therefore, $A_{\mathrm{out}}^{(1)} $ is replaced by the target amplitude, while the computed phase is retained:
\begin{equation}
\label{Gout}
G_{\mathrm{out}}^{(1)} = T \exp\Bigl\{i \phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{(1)}\Bigr\}\,,
\end{equation}
which defines the modified output field for the next step. An inverse Fourier transform is then applied to return to the input plane:
\begin{align}
\begin{split}
G_{\mathrm{in}}^{(1)}[p,q] &= \mathcal{F}^{-1}\Bigl\{G_{\mathrm{out}}^{(1)}[x,y] \Bigr\} \\&= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{x,y} G_{\mathrm{out}}^{(1)}[x,y] \exp\Bigl\{2\pi i (px + qy) /N \Bigr\} \\&= A_{\mathrm{in}}^{(1)} \exp\Bigl\{ i \phi_{\mathrm{in}}^{(1)}\Bigr\}\,,
\end{split}
\end{align}
where $A_{\mathrm{in}}^{(1)}$ is the resulting amplitude and $\phi_{\mathrm{in}}^{(1)}$ the updated phase at the input plane. To enforce consistency with the input beam profile, the amplitude is again replaced by the original $A_{\mathrm{in}}^{(0)}$, while the phase is retained:
\begin{equation}
E_{\mathrm{in}}^{(2)} = A_{\mathrm{in}}^{(0)} \exp\Bigl\{i \phi_{\mathrm{in}}^{(1)} \Bigr\}\,,
\end{equation}
which becomes the input for the next iteration. This cycle of forward and inverse Fourier transforms is repeated, alternating between imposing the correct amplitude in the input and output planes.

The algorithm continues until a suitable convergence criterion is met, which is based on the following error between the generated and target intensity profile:
\begin{equation}
\label{costfunction}
\epsilon^{(n)} =  \sum_{x,y} \left( \left( A_{\mathrm{out}}^{(n)}[x,y] \right)^2 - \left( T[x,y] \right)^2 \right)^2 \,.
\end{equation}
A common stopping condition is that the relative change in error falls below a set threshold:
\begin{equation}
\label{convergence}
\epsilon^{(n)} - \epsilon^{(n-1)} < 10^{-k}\,,
\end{equation}
for some chosen $k$. The GS algorithm is straightforward to implement and performs well in many applications, though it may stagnate in local minima and typically requires modifications (such as MRAF or OMRAF) to achieve improved performance for complex target patterns. \subsection{Weighted Gerchberg--Saxton}
The weighted Gerchberg--Saxton algorithm is an extension of the standard GS method, designed to simultaneously optimise the diffraction efficiency and uniformity of holographically generated optical trap arrays. This is achieved by introducing a spatially varying weight function that is iteratively updated to correct non-uniformities. Instead of applying the standard update rule given by \autoref{Gout} at the $n^{\mathrm{th}}$ iteration, the output field is modified as
\begin{equation}
\label{Gout2}
G_{\mathrm{out}}^{(n)} = T^{(n)} \exp\Bigl\{i \phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{(n)}\Bigr\}\,,
\end{equation}
where the target amplitude $T^{(n)}$ evolves according to
\begin{align}
T^{(n)} =
\begin{cases}
T & \text{if } n=1\,, \\
w^{(n)} T^{(n-1)} & \text{if } n>1\,.
\end{cases}
\end{align}
The weights $w^{(n)}[x,y]$ are defined by
\begin{equation}
w^{(n)}[x,y] = 
\begin{cases}
\sqrt{ \dfrac{\big\langle \left(A_{\mathrm{out}}^{(n)}[x,y]\right)^2 \big\rangle_\Omega}{\left(A_{\mathrm{out}}^{(n)}[x,y]\right)^2} } & \text{if } [x,y] \in \Omega\,, \\
1 & \text{otherwise}\,,
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
with the average intensity taken over the region $\Omega$ associated with the traps:
\begin{equation}
\big\langle \left(A_{\mathrm{out}}^{(n)}[x,y]\right)^2 \big\rangle_\Omega = \frac{1}{N_\Omega} \sum_{(x, y)\in\Omega} \left(A_{\mathrm{out}}^{(n)}(x, y)\right)^2\,,
\end{equation}
where $N_\Omega$ denotes the number of pixels used for the trapping sites.

\subsection{Mixed-region amplitude freedom}
A widely used method for generating holographic optical tweezers is the mixed region amplitude freedom algorithm, originally introduced in 2008~\cite{Pasienski2008}. It is an iterative Fourier transform algorithm that extends the conventional GS approach by introducing partial amplitude freedom in the output plane. This allows it to achieve improved accuracy and smoother intensity profiles, especially for complex target patterns. The key idea behind the MRAF algorithm is to divide the output plane into two regions: a signal region $\Omega_\mathrm{s}$ and a noise region $\Omega_\mathrm{n}$. The signal region contains the target intensity pattern along with a low-intensity buffer zone in which the amplitude is constrained to match the target while the phase remains free. Outside the signal region lies the noise region, where both amplitude and phase are left unconstrained, allowing light to be redistributed freely to improve convergence and reduce artefacts. Similar to the GSW approach, the MRAF algorithm modifies the output field update step in \autoref{Gout} by introducing an adaptive target $T^{(n)}$, as in \autoref{Gout2}. Via its definition in MRAF, however, it mixes the target amplitude in the signal region with the unconstrained field in the noise region:
\begin{equation}
\label{mrafmixing}
T^{(n)}(x, y) =
\begin{cases}
\sqrt{\frac{\alpha}{c_T}} T(x, y) & \text{if } (x, y) \in \Omega_\mathrm{s}\,, \\
\sqrt{\frac{1-\alpha}{c_{A,n}}}A^{(n)}_{\mathrm{out}}(x, y) & \text{if } (x, y) \in \Omega_\mathrm{n}\,,
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ is the mixing parameter that represents the fraction of optical power assigned to the signal region. The constants $c_T$ and $c_{A,n}$ ensure normalisation of the total intensity and are defined as:
\begin{align}
\begin{split}
c_T &= \sum_{[x,y]\in\Omega_\mathrm{s}} [T[x,y]]^2\,,\\
c_{A,n} &= \sum_{[x,y]\in\Omega_\mathrm{n}} [A^{(n)}_{\mathrm{out}}[x,y]]^2\,.
\end{split}
\end{align}
Figure \autoref{fig:mraf} shows a schematic representation of the MRAF algorithm. The phase pattern is iteratively updated in the hologram and then propagated through the Fourier transform to yield an intensity distribution that matches the target profile in the signal region. The periphery (noise region) is deliberately left unconstrained to absorb the light that does not contribute to the desired pattern.

The MRAF algorithm provides a strong balance between computational speed and accuracy. Compared to error-diffusion-based methods, MRAF achieves superior fidelity for large arrays~\cite{Holland2018}. Furthermore, we have observed that MRAF is at least $20$ times faster than conjugate gradient minimisation, making it highly suitable for real-time or large-scale implementations.

\subsection{Conjugate gradient minimisation}
As previously discussed, iterative Fourier transform algorithms are not true minimisation methods. In contrast, genuine minimisation approaches, such as direct search~\cite{Clark1996} or genetic algorithms~\cite{michalewicz1999genetic}, employ an explicit cost function to guide the optimisation process. However, these methods are computationally demanding, with longer runtimes and higher memory requirements. In the direct search method, the phase of each pixel is adjusted sequentially. After each modification, the cost function is evaluated and the change is accepted if the cost decreases. Although straightforward, this method is extremely slow, particularly when multiple phase levels are permitted. Genetic algorithms provide a more global optimisation strategy, operating on a population of phase patterns that evolve through selection and recombination, again guided by a cost function. These algorithms can be effective for simple binary-phase targets, but extending them to multi-level phase patterns drastically increases both memory usage and runtime. A more efficient alternative is gradient-based optimisation. The most straightforward example is the steepest descent method, which follows the local gradient of the cost function and progresses iteratively towards a minimum. While intuitive, it can perform poorly when the cost function landscape is ill-conditioned, causing the optimisation to take small, inefficient steps and converge slowly. The conjugate gradient (CG) method, which is most effective when the cost function is approximately quadratic near its optimum, addresses this problem by choosing directions that are conjugate with respect to the curvature of the cost function. Specifically, directions $ \vec{u} $ and $ \vec{v} $ are said to be mutually conjugate with respect to a symmetric positive-definite matrix $ A $ if $\vec{u}^\top A \vec{v} = 0$. In the context of minimising a quadratic cost function
\begin{equation}
f(\vec{x}) = \tfrac{1}{2} \vec{x}^\top A \vec{x} + \vec{b}^{\,\top} \vec{x} + c\,,
\end{equation}
the matrix $ A $ represents the Hessian of the cost function, which characterises its local curvature and anisotropy. In isotropic cases, all directions are equally steep, but in stretched landscapes such as $ f(x, y) = x^2 + 8y^2 $, efficient descent must account for this curvature. Importantly, the conjugate gradient method does not require explicit evaluation or storage of the Hessian matrix. Instead, it relies solely on gradient computations and recursively constructs conjugate search directions $\vec{p}_n$, making it more computationally efficient than methods requiring second derivatives. Starting with the initial steepest descent direction $\vec{g}_0$, we set $\vec{p}_0 = \vec{g}_0$. Subsequent directions are computed via
\begin{equation}
\vec{p}_{n} = \vec{g}_{n} + \beta_{n} \vec{p}_{n-1}\,,
\end{equation}
with the update factor
\begin{equation}
\beta_{n} = \frac{\vec{g}_{n}^\top \vec{g}_{n}}{\vec{g}_{n-1}^\top \vec{g}_{n-1}}\,,
\end{equation}
where $\vec{g}_n$ denotes the gradient of the cost function at step $n$. This update factor $\beta_n$, often referred to as the Fletcher--Reeves coefficient~\cite{Fletcher1964}, retains a form of memory by scaling the influence of the previous search direction based on the relative steepness of the cost function. If the new gradient $\vec{g}_n$ is relatively small in magnitude, $\beta_n$ reduces the contribution of the previous direction, effectively favouring steepest descent near convergence. An alternative to the Fletcher--Reeves update is the Polak--Ribière--Polyak (PRP) method, where the update factor is given by
\begin{equation}
\beta_n = \frac{(\vec{g}_n - \vec{g}_{n-1})^{\!\top} \vec{g}_n}{\vec{g}_{n-1}^\top \vec{g}_{n-1}}\,.
\end{equation}
This expression allows the search direction to adapt more flexibly to the local curvature of the cost function, often improving practical performance in non-quadratic landscapes~\cite{Polyak1969,M2AN_1969__3_1_35_0}. However, the PRP method can suffer from poor convergence, potentially cycling indefinitely even when exact line searches are used~\cite{Powell1984}. To overcome this issue, Powell proposed a modified version known as the PRP$^+$ method~\cite{Powell1984}, which enforces a non-negativity constraint on the update factor:
\begin{equation}
\beta_n = \max\Bigg\{0, \frac{(\vec{g}_n - \vec{g}_{n-1})^{\!\top} \vec{g}_n}{\vec{g}_{n-1}^{\top} \vec{g}_{n-1}} \Bigg\}\,,
\end{equation}
thereby enhancing the method's global convergence behaviour. In modern applications with non-linear optimisation problems, some of the most effective conjugate gradient algorithms adaptively select the update formula for $\beta_n$ during the optimisation process to improve convergence properties~\cite{hager2006survey}. In practice, we find that performing a few initial steps of steepest descent before switching to a conjugate gradient direction further enhances convergence. The steepest descent method follows the local gradient direction at each step, which results in oscillatory and inefficient progress on anisotropic landscapes. In contrast, the conjugate gradient method selects search directions that account for the curvature and converges to the exact minimum in at most $ n $ iterations for a quadratic cost function in an $ n $-dimensional space. This is not the case for non-quadratic cost functions, where convergence typically requires more than $n$ steps.

In our implementation for phase holography, the goal is to optimise the output intensity pattern to match a desired target. The controllable variable in this optimisation is the input phase pattern, while the input amplitude is kept fixed. To iteratively update the input phase $\phi_\mathrm{in}^{(n)}[p,q]$, we evaluate the gradient of the cost function $\epsilon^{(n+1)}$ (\autoref{costfunction}), which depends on the resulting output amplitude $A_\mathrm{out}^{(n+1)}[x,y]$ (see \autoref{forwardfourier}):
\begin{align}
\label{gradientdef}
\begin{split}
\frac{\partial \epsilon^{(n+1)}}{\partial \phi_\mathrm{in}^{(n)}[p,q]} = 4\Re {i \left(E_\mathrm{in}^{(n+1)}[p,q]\right)^{\!*} X[p,q] } \,,
\end{split}
\end{align}
where
\begin{equation}
X[p,q]\coloneqq \mathcal{F}^{-1}\Bigl\{  E_\mathrm{out}^{(n+1)} [x,y] \left( [T[x,y]]^2 - \big\lvert  E_\mathrm{out}^{(n+1)}[x,y] \big\rvert^2 \right)\Bigr\} \,.
\end{equation}
For use in the conjugate gradient minimisation algorithm, we stack all partial derivatives from \autoref{gradientdef} into a single vector $\vec{g}_n$. For the initial iteration ($n = 0$), the conjugate direction is taken as the steepest descent direction ($\vec{p}_0 = \vec{g}_0$) and the cost function is minimised along this direction using Brent’s method. For all subsequent iterations ($n \geq 1$), the new search direction $\vec{p}_n$ is computed from the current and previous gradients, $\vec{g}_n$ and $\vec{g}_{n-1}$, together with the previous search direction $\vec{p}_{n-1}$. A line search is again performed using Brent’s method and used to update the input phase ($\phi_\mathrm{in}^{(n)}[p,q] \rightarrow \phi_\mathrm{in}^{(n+1)}[p,q]$), after which the gradient is recomputed accordingly. This process is iterated until convergence is achieved as defined in \autoref{convergence}.

\section{IFTA benchmarking}
In this section, we analyse the simulated and measured performance of various iterative Fourier transform algorithms (IFTAs) to generate uniform optical trap arrays with high efficiency and fast convergence. We focus on a fixed $4 \times 4$ trap array and assess each algorithm according to three key criteria: the total optical power delivered to the traps (to ensure efficient use of optical power), the uniformity of trap intensities and the convergence rate of the algorithm. To quantify trap uniformity, we use the coefficient of variation
\begin{equation}
\label{coeffofvariation}
c_\mathrm{v} = \frac{1}{\langle I_n \rangle} \sqrt{ \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^{N} \left( I_n - \langle I_n \rangle \right)^2 }\,,
\end{equation}
where $N$ is the number of traps, $I_n$ is the intensity in trap number $n$ and $\langle I_n \rangle$ denotes the mean trap intensity across the array. This metric is the standard deviation normalised by the mean, with lower values indicating better uniformity. We benchmark eight variants within the IFTA-class, including the standard GS, the MRAF algorithm with different trapping-region power constraints ($\alpha\in\{ 0.8,0.9,0.99\}$ as mixing parameters in \autoref{mrafmixing}), GSW and a hybrid GSW--MRAF method, where GSW weighting is applied prior to the MRAF update step with the same respective power constraints.

The GS algorithm produces a moderately homogeneous intensity distribution, with a mid-range coefficient of variation. Introducing the MRAF algorithm with a high mixing parameter of $\alpha = 0.99$ results in a dramatic degradation of uniformity, as evident from both the heatmap and the significantly increased value of $c_\mathrm{v}$. This indicates that, without weighting, MRAF at high $\alpha$ is counterproductive for producing uniform trap arrays. Reducing $\alpha$ to more moderate values substantially improves uniformity. The weighted approach using GSW performs exceptionally well, as it yields the most homogeneous distribution with the lowest coefficient of variation across all methods, while still preserving a high total intensity---making it a highly favourable option. The hybrid GSW--MRAF method further demonstrates marked improvements over unweighted MRAF. Across all mixing parameters, the hybrid approach reduces the coefficient of variation by \num{2}--\num{3} orders of magnitude. In particular, even in the case of $\alpha = 0.99$, this method becomes a viable candidate, achieving the highest total intensity among the weighted MRAF variants while maintaining acceptable uniformity.

In summary, GSW offers the best overall performance, combining excellent uniformity with high trap intensity. The hybrid GSW--MRAF variants offer flexible alternatives with improved robustness over pure MRAF.

These images provide a qualitative assessment of the uniformity and brightness of the resulting trap arrays. All methods produce the intended $4 \times 4$ grid of traps, but also exhibit some ghost traps which are not located at the positions of the calculated artefacts in \autoref{calculated4by4}. Instead, they appear shifted relative to the main trapping grid, which is consistent with additional diffraction orders introduced by the SLM. Because the SLM is composed of an array of discrete pixels, its phase modulation acts like a sampled aperture, leading to the formation of multiple diffraction orders in the far field, which are effectively shifted replicas of the original pattern~\cite{Agour2009}. Furthermore, the inactive areas between pixels leave a fraction of the reflected light unmodulated, giving rise to a prominent central bright spot---known as the zero-order or DC term. To avoid interference, the trapping grid is positioned clear of the zero-order diffraction spot, allowing the latter to be masked if necessary.

Across all methods, the measured coefficients of variation are significantly larger than those obtained from the calculated hologram intensity distributions, but consistently remain within the same order of magnitude. This reduced uniformity arises primarily from experimental imperfections such as optical aberrations introduced by the SLM and other components in the beam path. For most methods, a consistent trend is visible, where lower intensity values are found in the bottom rows of the grid, corresponding to traps positioned further from the optical centre. This drop-off is in agreement with previous observations that showed traps located away from the centre required more optical power to achieve comparable trapping efficiency~\cite{Schymik2022}. The underlying cause is attributed to optical aberrations and the finite diffraction-limited field of view of the aspheric lenses used in high-NA imaging systems. Importantly, this non-uniformity does not impose a fundamental limit. It can be compensated for by incorporating aberration correction directly onto the SLM phase pattern~\cite{imr2010,Kim2019} and by using iterative feedback techniques based on site-resolved intensity or fluorescence measurements~\cite{Tamura2016,Schymik2022}. Thus, while choosing the right kinoform generation method is important, optical imperfections ultimately dominate in the absence of feedback correction. This is especially evident when comparing the calculated and measured performance. The relative improvements from the unweighted methods to their weighted counterparts are still discernible in \autoref{measured4by4grid}, though diminished in scale when compared to those in \autoref{calculated4by4grid}. Of all tested methods, the GSW and hybrid GSW--MRAF algorithm with $\alpha = 0.9$ provide the best measured trade-off between site-to-site uniformity and efficient light delivery to the traps.

When factoring in the increased computational complexity and slower convergence of CG minimisation, the theoretical improvements in cost function value are not sufficient to justify its use in practice.

While conjugate gradient methods offer flexibility through customisable cost functions, their comparatively longer runtimes and limited improvements in trap homogeneity or power efficiency make them less compelling for practical implementation in dipole trap array generation. In contrast, GSW-based approaches offer a better balance between speed, efficiency and uniformity. Finally, it is important to note that in experiments, trap uniformity is ultimately limited by optical aberrations and other imperfections. These must be corrected using feedback based on in-situ measurements to realise the theoretical performance observed in simulation.
