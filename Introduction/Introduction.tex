\blockquote[\begin{flushright}\vspace{-0.0\baselineskip}\textsc{— E. Schrödinger (1887--1961)} \end{flushright}][]{``\emph{I would not call [entanglement] one but rather the characteristic trait of quantum mechanics, the one that enforces its entire	departure from classical lines of thought.}"} 
\vspace{2em}
Since the early days of quantum mechanics, entanglement has stood out as one of its most striking and counter-intuitive features. Originally described by Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen as a paradox~\cite{PhysRev.47.777}, it has reshaped our understanding of physical reality---from Bell’s theorem to modern, loophole-free tests of local realism~\cite{Bell1964,Clauser1969,Hensen2015,Giustina2015,Shalm2015}. Entanglement is now understood not merely as a peculiarity, but as a fundamental aspect of nature---and a resource to be harnessed. It underpins many of today's most powerful applications of quantum information science, including quantum tele\-por\-ta\-tion~\cite{Bennett1993}, entanglement swapping~\cite{ukowski1993} and device-independent cryptography~\cite{Acn2007}.

\section{From entanglement to quantum networks}
These developments have led to the notion of a quantum network: a distributed architecture in which distant nodes exchange and process quantum information mediated by entanglement~\cite{Cirac1997,Kimble2008,Perseguers2013,Wehner2018}. Such networks promise secure communication through quantum key distribution (QKD)~\cite{Bennett1987,PhysRevLett.67.661}, entanglement-enhanced quantum sensing and clock synchronisation~\cite{Giovannetti2011,Jozsa2000,Kmr2014}, as well as the interconnection of modular quantum processors for distributed computation~\cite{Denchev2008}. Demonstrations over metropolitan scales already illustrate feasibility---including deployed QKD networks~\cite{Peev2009,Sasaki2011,Stucki2011} and satellite-assisted entanglement distribution over thousands of kilometres~\cite{Vallone2015,Yin2017}.

\section{Photonic links and quantum nodes}
Photons are the natural carriers of quantum information over long distances, owing to their high propagation speed and resilience to decoherence. However, they require an efficient interface to stationary systems where information can be stored and processed. Crucially, such end nodes must include long-lived quantum memories, support high-fidelity local operations and couple efficiently to photonic channels at the relevant wavelength\footnote{Telecom bands for optical fibre-based networks such that the photon transmission probability decreases by only \SI{0.2}{\deci B\per\kilo\meter}~\cite{Lines1984}.}. Multiple experimental platforms are being developed to meet these requirements. Progress is marked by demonstrations of photon-mediated entanglement between distant matter qubits, which include semiconductor quantum dots~\cite{Delteil2016,Stockill2017}, colour centres in diamond~\cite{Bernien2013}, trapped ions~\cite{Moehring2007,Blatt2008} and neutral atoms~\cite{rempe2012,Hofmann2012,vanLeent2022}. Each system offers distinct advantages and limitations in coherence, optical properties and scalability. Neutral atoms, for instance, offer long-lived internal states and benefit from well-established cooling and trapping techniques. Moreover, their electronic transitions---set by atomic structure---are largely free from material-induced inhomogeneous broadening and spectral diffusion common to solid-state emitters, which benefits photon indistinguishability required for entanglement protocols. While trapped ions offer superb coherence and gate fidelities, scaling beyond modest system sizes in a single device is challenging~\cite{Kielpinski2002}. By contrast, neutral atoms provide a natural route to scalability: optical tweezer arrays enable deterministic preparation of large, defect-free registers with single-site control and readout~\cite{Manetsch2024}---enabling fully programmable quantum processors operating with up to \num{280} physical qubits~\cite{Bluvstein2023}.

\section{Resonators for light-matter interfacing}
Long-distance networking requires an interface that faithfully swaps quantum states between photonic and stationary qubits within their coherence times~\cite{Reiserer2015}. Yet even with diffraction-limited focusing, the interaction probability of a single atom with a resonant photon in free space is limited by finite solid-angle coverage and imperfect mode matching~\cite{Leuchs2013}. Quantum network nodes therefore demand stronger spatio-temporal confinement of the electromagnetic field, which can be achieved with optical resonators. By recycling the field, a resonator lets the emitter repeatedly meet the same photon, enhancing emission into and absorption from a designated mode. The first unidirectional atom-photon interface---mapping the quantum state of a single atom onto a propagating single photon---was realised with \ce{^{87}Rb} atoms traversing a high-finesse Fabry--Pérot resonator~\cite{Wilk2007}. Since then, resonator-based interfaces have enabled spin-photon entanglement with trapped atoms~\cite{Weber2009}, storage of single photons~\cite{rempe2012}, quantum teleportation between remote atoms~\cite{Nolleke2013,Langenfeld2021}, photon-mediated quantum gates~\cite{Reiserer2014,Daiss2021} and quantum repeater nodes for unconditionally secure QKD~\cite{Langenfeld2021b}. Taken together, these advances have established trapped neutral atoms---paired with optical resonators---as an excellent platform for quantum networks.

\section{Thesis outline}
In this work we pursue the resonator route with neutral-atom Fabry–Pérot or fibre-integrated cavities (see \autoref{chap:TheoryandDesignofanOpticalResonator}).

This thesis focuses on neutral atoms coupled to a high-finesse optical cavity, aiming to realize a deterministic atom–photon interface. By combining single-atom optical dipole traps with cavity quantum electrodynamics (CQED), we explore a route toward efficient single-photon generation and atom–photon entanglement, both essential primitives for quantum networking.
