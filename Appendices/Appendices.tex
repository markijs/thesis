\chapter{Rotating Frame Transformation}
\label{rotatingframeapp}
Transforming a Hamiltonian to the rotating frame simplifies the time dependence of the corresponding quantum states. Consider for example a Hamiltonian that can be decomposed into a time-independent part $\hat{\mathcal{H}}_0$ and a time-dependent part $\hat{\mathcal{H}}_1(t)$ as $\hat{\mathcal{H}}(t) = \hat{\mathcal{H}}_0 + \hat{\mathcal{H}}_1(t)$. The system dynamics are governed by the Schrödinger equation:
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial}{\partial t} \lvert \psi(t) \rangle &= -\frac{i}{\hbar} \hat{\mathcal{H}}(t) \lvert \psi(t) \rangle\,,
\end{align}
which has the following formal solution:
\begin{align}
\lvert \psi(t) \rangle &= \hat{U}(t, t_0) \lvert \psi(t_0) \rangle\,.
\end{align}
The unitary time evolution operator $\hat{U}(t, t_0)$ is given in terms of the following time-ordered exponential:
\begin{align}
\hat{U}(t, t_0) &= \mathcal{T} \exp { -\frac{i}{\hbar} \int_{t_0}^{t} \hat{\mathcal{H}}(\tau) \, d\tau }\,.
\end{align}
Now, instead of working with $\lvert \psi(t) \rangle$, we define a new state vector $\lvert \phi(t) \rangle$:
\begin{align}
\lvert \phi(t) \rangle &= \hat{R}(t) \lvert \psi(t) \rangle\,,
\end{align}
where $\hat{R}(t)$ represents an arbitrary (unitary) rotation operator, such that $\hat{R}^{\dagger}(t) \hat{R}(t) = \hat{R}(t) \hat{R}^{\dagger}(t) = \mathbb{I}$. Now let us find the time dependence of this newly defined state vector, which essentially is the left-hand side of the Schrödinger equation:
\begin{align}
\begin{split}
\frac{\partial}{\partial t} \lvert \phi(t) \rangle &= \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( \hat{R}(t) \lvert \psi(t) \rangle \right) \\
&= \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \hat{R}(t) \right) \lvert \psi(t) \rangle + \hat{R}(t) \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \lvert \psi(t) \rangle \\
&= \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \hat{R}(t) \right) \lvert \psi(t) \rangle - \frac{i}{\hbar} \hat{R}(t) \hat{\mathcal{H}}(t) \lvert \psi(t) \rangle \\
&= \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \hat{R}(t) \right) \hat{R}^{\dagger}(t) \lvert \phi(t) \rangle - \frac{i}{\hbar} \hat{R}(t) \hat{\mathcal{H}}(t) \hat{R}^{\dagger}(t) \lvert \phi(t) \rangle \\
&= -\frac{i}{\hbar} \left( i \hbar \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \hat{R}(t) \right) \hat{R}^{\dagger}(t) + \hat{R}(t) \hat{\mathcal{H}}(t) \hat{R}^{\dagger}(t) \right) \lvert \phi(t) \rangle \\
&= -\frac{i}{\hbar} \hat{\mathcal{H}}'(t) \lvert \phi(t) \rangle\,.
\end{split}
\end{align}
In the final line, we observe that our new state vector $\lvert \phi(t) \rangle$ obeys the Schrödinger equation with a modified Hamiltonian $\hat{\mathcal{H}}'(t)$. The transformed Hamiltonian in the rotating frame is thus given by:
\begin{align}
\hat{\mathcal{H}}'(t) = i \hbar \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \hat{R}(t) \right) \hat{R}^{\dagger}(t) + \hat{R}(t) \hat{\mathcal{H}}(t) \hat{R}^{\dagger}(t)\,.
\end{align}
A particularly useful choice for the rotation operator is $\hat{R}(t) = \hat{U}_0^\dagger(t, t_0)$, where $\hat{U}_0(t, t_0)$ denotes the unitary evolution operator for the time-independent part of $\hat{\mathcal{H}}(t)$:
\begin{align}
\hat{U}_0(t, t_0) = \exp{-\frac{i}{\hbar} (t - t_0) \hat{\mathcal{H}}_0}\,.
\end{align}
In this case, the transformed Hamiltonian acquires the following form:
\begin{align}
\hat{\mathcal{H}}'(t) = \hat{U}_0^{\dagger}(t, t_0) \hat{\mathcal{H}}_1(t) \hat{U}_0(t, t_0)\,,
\end{align}
which is also referred to as the Hamiltonian in the interaction picture.

\chapter{Spherical Tensors}
\label{sphericaltensors}
Spherical tensors are described using the set of spherical basis vectors $\{\hat{\mathrm{e}}_1, \hat{\mathrm{e}}_0, \hat{\mathrm{e}}_{-1}\}$ that are defined as:
\begin{align}
\begin{split}
\hat{\mathrm{e}}_{\pm 1} &\coloneqq \frac{\mp(\hat{\mathrm{e}}_x\pm i \hat{\mathrm{e}}_y)}{\sqrt{2}}\\ \hat{\mathrm{e}}_0&\coloneqq\hat{\mathrm{e}}_z\,,
\end{split}
\end{align}
in terms of the Cartesian unit vectors. The contravariant components $A^q$ of a vector $\vec{A}$ are:
\begin{align}
A^q=\hat{\mathrm{e}}_q^*\cdot \vec{A}\,,
\end{align}
which allow us to decompose $\vec{A}$ in the spherical basis as:
\begin{align}
\vec{A}=\sum_q A^q \hat{\mathrm{e}}_q\,.
\end{align}
In the dual basis, which consists of the basis vectors $\hat{\mathrm{e}}^q=\hat{\mathrm{e}}_q^*$ such that $\hat{\mathrm{e}}^q\hat{\mathrm{e}}_{q'}=\delta_{qq'}$, the covariant components of $\vec{A}$ are:
\begin{align}
A_q = \hat{\mathrm{e}}_q\cdot \vec{A}\,,
\end{align} 
such that the decomposition reads:
\begin{align}
\vec{A}=\sum_q A_q \hat{\mathrm{e}}^q\,.
\end{align}
Note that $\hat{\mathrm{e}}^q=\hat{\mathrm{e}}_q^*=(-1)^q\hat{\mathrm{e}}_{-q}$, which gives rise to various notations for the decomposition of $\vec{A}$ and the fact that $A^0=A_0$ and $A^{\pm 1}= -A_{\mp 1}$. Using different representations, the inner product of two vectors $\vec{A}$ and $\vec{B}$ in the spherical basis can be written as:
\begin{align}
\begin{split}
\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}= A^q \hat{\mathrm{e}}_q B_{q'} \hat{\mathrm{e}}^{q'} = A^q B_q &= A^1 B_1 + A^0 B_0 + A^{-1} B_{-1} \\&= -A^1 B^{-1} + A^0 B^0 - A^{-1} B^1 \\&= \sum_q (-1)^q A^q B^{-q} \\&= \sum_q (-1)^q A_q B_{-q}\,.
\end{split}
\end{align}
When it comes to converting Cartesian tensor components into their spherical tensor counterparts, the approach depends on the rank of the tensor. A rank-$0$ tensor $T$ will simply have one spherical tensor component $T_0^{(0)}=T$. For a Cartesian rank-$1$ tensor $T_\mu$, the spherical tensor components $T^{(1)}_q$ will be the covariant components $T_q$ as described above. The reason is that the covariant components rotate in the same way as the spherical harmonics, as opposed to the contravariant elements. For a rank-$2$ tensor $T_{\mu\nu}$, the trick involves writing the tensor as the direct product of two rank-$1$ tensors: $T_{\mu\nu}=A_\mu B_\nu$. The spherical tensor components $T_q^{(k)}$ can then be obtained by coupling the spherical components $A^{(1)}_{q_1}$ and $B^{(1)}_{q_2}$ like angular momenta via the Clebsch–Gordan coefficients, since the spherical tensor components were defined to transform in the same way as spherical harmonics:
\begin{align}
T_q^{(k)}=\sum_{\substack{q_1q_2\\
		q_1+q_2=q}} T^{(k_1)}_{q_1} T^{(k_2)}_{q_2} \langle k_1\, q_1 ; k_2\, q_2 \vert k\, q \rangle\,,
\end{align}
where $\vert k_1 - k_2 \vert \le k \le k_1 + k_2$ and $\langle k_1\, q_1 ; k_2\, q_2 \vert k\, q \rangle$ represents the Clebsch–Gordan coefficient. Substituting $T^{(k_1)}_{q_1}=A^{(1)}_{q_1}$ and $T^{(k_2)}_{q_2}=B^{(1)}_{q_2}$ allows us to construct all spherical tensor components $\{T_0^{(0)}, T_{\pm q}^{(1)}, T_{\pm q}^{(2)}\}$. The rank-$0$ combination, for instance, is given by:
\begin{align}
T_0^{(0)}=-\sum_{q=-1}^{1} \frac{(-1)^q}{\sqrt{3}}A^{(1)}_q B^{(1)}_{-q}=-\frac{\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}}{\sqrt{3}}\,,
\end{align}
rewritten in terms of the inner product of the vectors $\vec{A}$ and $\vec{B}$. Similarly, the rank-$1$ combination can be evaluated, giving rise to the following three components:
\begin{align}
\begin{split}
T_1^{(1)} &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(A_1B_0-A_0B_1)\,,\\
T_0^{(1)} &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(A_1B_{-1}-A_{-1}B_1)\,,\\
T_{-1}^{(1)} &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(A_0B_{-1}-A_{-1}B_0)\,,
\end{split}
\end{align}
which can be summarised in terms of the spherical components of the cross product $\vec{A}\times\vec{B}$:
\begin{align}
T_q^{1}=\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}(\vec{A}\times\vec{B})_q\,.
\end{align}
Lastly, the rank-$2$ components are:
\begin{align}
\begin{split}
T_{\pm 2}^{(2)} &= A_{\pm 1}B_{\pm 1}\,,\\
T_{\pm 1}^{(2)} &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(A_{\pm 1}B_{0}+A_{0}B_{\pm 1})\,,\\
T_{0}^{(2)} &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}(A_1B_{-1}+2A_0B_0+A_{-1}B_1)\,.
\end{split}
\end{align}
All of these spherical tensor components can subsequently be written in terms of the Cartesian tensor components $T_{\mu\nu}$ by expressing all instances of $A_{q_1}$ and $B_{q_2}$ in the Cartesian basis:
\begin{align}
\label{sphericalintermsofcartesian}
\begin{split}
T_0^{(0)}&=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\left[T_{xx}+T_{yy}+T_{zz}\right]\,,\\
T_{\pm 1}^{(1)} &=\frac{1}{2}\left[T_{zx}-T_{xz}\pm i(T_{zy}-T_{yz})\right]\,,\\
T_0^{(1)} &=\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}\left[T_{xy}-T_{yx}\right]\,,\\
T_{\pm 2}^{(2)} &= \frac{1}{2}\left[T_{xx}-T_{yy}\pm i (T_{xy}+T_{yx})\right]\,,\\
T_{\pm 1}^{(2)} &= \frac{1}{2}\left[\mp (T_{xz}+T_{zx}) - i (T_{yz}+T_{zy})\right]\,,\\
T_{0}^{(2)} &= -\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\left[T_{xx}+T_{yy}-2T_{zz}\right]\,.
\end{split}
\end{align}
We note that the rank-$0$, rank-$1$ and rank-$2$ spherical components relate directly to the isotropic, antisymmetric and symmetric traceless parts of the tensor, respectively. An important result from representation theory is the Wigner-Eckart theorem, which states that, given a tensor operator $T^{(k)}$, the matrix elements of its spherical component $T^{(k)}_q$ between two angular momentum eigenstates $\vert j\, m\rangle$ and $\vert j'\, m'\rangle$ can be decomposed as:
\begin{align}
\label{wignereckart}
\langle j\, m \vert  T^{(k)}_q \vert j'\, m' \rangle = (-1)^{2k}\langle j \| T^{(k)} \| j' \rangle \langle j\, m \vert j'\, m'; k\, q \rangle\,.
\end{align}
This factorisation involves a Clebsch–Gordan coefficient $\langle j\, m \vert j'\, m'; k\, q \rangle$ that encapsulates all orientation dependence of the matrix element and the so-called reduced matrix element $\langle j \| T^{(k)} \| j' \rangle$, which does not depend on $m$, $m′$, nor $q$ and is therefore orientation independent. The inverse relation is:
\begin{align}
\label{inversewigner}
\langle j \| T^{(k)} \| j' \rangle = (-1)^{2k}\sum_{m'q} \langle j\, m \vert  T^{(k)}_q \vert j'\, m' \rangle \langle j\, m \vert j'\, m'; k\, q \rangle\,.
\end{align}
Through the use of the Wigner-Eckart theorem, one can derive the following relation involving the conjugated reduced matrix element:
\begin{align}
\label{ccreducedmatrixelement}
\langle j' \| T^{(k)} \| j \rangle = (-1)^{j'-j}\sqrt{\frac{2j+1}{2j'+1}}\langle j \| T^{(k)} \| j' \rangle^*\,.
\end{align}
Now assume that we have a reduced matrix element defined as $\langle j \| T^{(k)} \| j' \rangle \coloneqq \langle j_1\, j_2\, j \| T^{(k)} \| j_1'\,j_2'\,j' \rangle$ between angular momentum states of $\hat{J}=\hat{J}_1+\hat{J}_2$. If $T^{(k)}$ only acts on the states that correspond to $\hat{J}_1$ (and not $\hat{J}_2$), then $\langle j \| T^{(k)} \| j' \rangle$ can be written in terms of a reduced matrix element that involves only the quantum numbers $j_1$ and $j_1'$:
\begin{align}
\label{reducedmatrixelementonecomponent}
\begin{split}
\langle j \| T^{(k)} \| j' \rangle &= \delta_{j_2 j_2'} (-1)^{j'+j_1+k+j_2}\sqrt{(2j'+1)(2j_1+1)}\\ &\hspace{8em}\times\tj{j_1}{j_1'}{k}{j'}{j}{j_2} \langle j_1 \| T^{(k)} \| j_1' \rangle \,.
\end{split}
\end{align}
For a tensor product $T^{(k)}=U^{(k_1)}V^{(k_2)}$ where both $U^{(k_1)}$ and $V^{(k_2)}$ act on the same angular momentum space, the reduced matrix element of $T^{(k)}$ can be decomposed as follows
\begin{align}
\label{splitreducedmatrixelement}
\begin{split}
\langle j \| T^{(k)} \| j' \rangle = (-1)^{k+j+j'}\sum_{j''}\sqrt{(2j''+1)(2k+1)} \tj{k_1}{k_2}{k}{j'}{j}{j''} \\ \times \langle j \| U^{(k_1)} \| j'' \rangle \langle j'' \| V^{(k_2)} \| j' \rangle\,.
\end{split}
\end{align}
When it comes to Wigner-6j symbols, there are a few useful relations that we will discuss next. The first is the orthogonality relation:
\begin{align}
\label{orthogonality6j}
& \sum_j(2 j+1)\left(2 j''+1\right)\tj{j_1}{j_2}{j}{j_3}{j_4}{j'}\tj{j_1}{j_2}{j}{j_3}{j_4}{j''}=\delta_{j' j''}\,.
\end{align}
The second relation is the Biedenharn–Elliott sum rule:
\begin{align}
\begin{split}
&\tj{j_1}{j_2}{j_{12}}{j_3}{j_{123}}{j_{23}}\tj{j_{23}}{j_1}{j_{123}}{j_4}{j}{j_{14}}\\ &\hspace{3em}=\sum_{j_{124}}(-1)^{j_1+j_2+j_3+j_4+j_{12}+j_{23}+j_{14}+j_{123}+j_{124}+j}\left(2 j_{124}+1\right) \\
&\hspace{3em}\times\tj{j_3}{j_2}{j_{23}}{j_{14}}{j}{j_{124}} \tj{j_2}{j_1}{j_{12}}{j_4}{j_{124}}{j_{14}} \tj{j_3}{j_{12}}{j_{123}}{j_4}{j}{j_{124}}\,,
\end{split}
\end{align}
which, for a particular choice of quantum numbers, can be written in the following alternative form:
\begin{align}
\label{elliottrule}
\begin{split}
& \sum_{F'}(-1)^{F'}\left(2 F'+1\right)\tj{1}{1}{2}{F}{F}{F'}\tj{J}{J'}{1}{F'}{F}{I}^2\\[0.5em]
&\hspace{3em}=(-1)^{-\left(2 J+J'+2 F+I\right)}\tj{1}{1}{2}{J}{J}{J'}\tj{J}{J}{2}{F}{F}{I}\,,
\end{split}
\end{align}
where we have used the fact that the 6-j symbol is invariant under any permutation of the columns and under the exchange of upper and lower arguments in any two columns.

\autoref{fig:entangle} illustrates a particular entangling scheme in which two single \ce{^87Rb} atoms are coupled to optical cavities and prepared in the $\ket{F=2,m_F=0}$ state of the $5\mathrm{S}_{\sfrac{1}{2}}$ manifold. The cavity axis defines the quantisation direction, as shown in \autoref{fig:cavity}. Both right- ($\sigma^+$) and left-circular ($\sigma^-$) polarisation modes are supported by the resonator that couples $\ket{F=1}$ in the ground state to $\ket{F'=1}$ of the $5\mathrm{P}_{\sfrac{3}{2}}$ excited level. A $\pi$-polarised laser resonant with the $\ket{F=2}\leftrightarrow \ket{F'=1}$ transition drives a vacuum-stimulated Raman adiabatic passage to $\ket{\downarrow}_z \coloneqq \ket{F=1,m_F=-1}$ or $\ket{\uparrow}_z \coloneqq \ket{F=1,m_F=1}$ in the ground state, accompanied by the emission of a $\sigma^+$ or $\sigma^-$ photon, respectively. This prepares each atom-photon system in the following state:
\begin{equation}
\color{black} \ket{\Psi}_\mathrm{ap} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \big( \ket{\downarrow}_z\!\ket{\sigma^+} + \ket{\uparrow}_z\!\ket{\sigma^-} \! \big) \,.
\end{equation}

\section{Stochastic cavity loading}
\label{app:stochasticcavityloading}
For a total number of atoms $N$ in a control volume $V$, the probability $P(N,n,\alpha)$ that $n$ atoms occupy the effective interaction volume $\delta V$ of the cavity field is given by the binomial distribution:
\begin{equation}
\label{binomial}
P(N,n,\alpha)=\frac{N!}{\left(N-n\right)!\,n!}\,\alpha^n\left(1-\alpha\right)^{N-n}\,,
\end{equation}
where $\alpha=\sfrac{\delta V}{V}$. A quantity of interest is the following ratio:
\begin{equation}
\label{successratio}
\chi=\frac{P(1,N,\alpha) P(1,N,\alpha)}{2 P(2,N,\alpha) P(0,N,\alpha)}\,,
\end{equation}
which represents the success ratio of single atom loading in each of two identical cavities versus double loading in one of two identical cavities. \autoref{binomial} allows us to write $\chi$ in terms of the number of atoms per volume:
\begin{equation}
\chi(N)=\frac{N}{N-1}\,.
\end{equation}
In an experiment that involves stochastic cavity loading, which is the case for an atomic fountain, $N\gg1$, which implies that $\chi(N)\approx1$. This means that double loading of one cavity is as likely to occur as single atom loading in both cavities. Thus, one cannot be certain about the loading configuration in the event of a two-photon detection.

\chapter{Modal Analysis of a Linear Mechanical System}
\label{app:modalanalysis}
The equations of motion for a linear mechanical system with multiple degrees of freedom are coupled through its mass, damping, and stiffness matrices, making an analytical solution difficult to obtain directly. The goal of modal analysis is to find a coordinate transformation into modal coordinates in which the equations become decoupled. The result is a set of single-degree-of-freedom systems, each representing an individual vibrational mode. In this appendix, we discuss the decoupling procedure, derive the resulting system response in the frequency domain and introduce the concept of effective modal mass to quantify the relative contribution of each mode to the overall dynamic response.

\section{Equations of motion}
\label{eqsofmotion}
We begin with the general equation of motion for a linear mechanical system with $n$ degrees of freedom:
\begin{equation}
\label{ndogeq}
M  \ddot{\vec{x}}(t) + C  \dot{\vec{x}}(t) + K  \vec{x}(t) = \vec{f}(t),
\end{equation}
where $M$ is the mass matrix, $C$ the damping matrix, $K$ the stiffness matrix, $\vec{x}(t)$ the displacement vector and $\vec{f}(t)$ the external force vector. In general, $M$, $C$, and $K$ are symmetric but not diagonal, so the equations are coupled across all degrees of freedom. To decouple them we first consider the undamped system in the absence of external forces:
\begin{equation}
M \ddot{\vec{x}}(t) + K \vec{x}(t) = \vec{0}\,.
\end{equation}
We then seek harmonic solutions of the form $\vec{x}(t) = \vec{\phi} \, \mathrm{e}^{i \omega t}$, where $\vec{\phi}$ is a mode shape vector and $\omega$ the angular frequency. Substitution yields:
\begin{equation}
\bigl( K - \omega^{2} M \bigr)  \vec{\phi} = \vec{0}\,,
\end{equation}
which is the generalised eigenvalue problem. Non-trivial solutions exist only when $\det(K - \omega^{2} M)=0$, an equation which yields $n$ discrete natural frequencies $\omega_{i}$ and their associated mode shape eigenvectors $\vec{\phi}_{i}$ for $i \in \{ 1, \dots, n\}$. Collecting the eigenvectors as columns yields the following modal matrix:
\begin{equation}
\Phi = \begin{bmatrix}  \vec{\phi}_{1} & \vec{\phi}_{2} & \cdots & \vec{\phi}_{n} \end{bmatrix}\,.
\end{equation}
The eigenvectors satisfy the following orthogonality relations with respect to both $M$ and $K$:
\begin{equation}
\vec{\phi}_{i}^{\,\top} \!M \vec{\phi}_{j} = 0\,, \qquad \vec{\phi}_{i}^{\,\top} \!K \vec{\phi}_{j} = 0 \qquad \text{for} \qquad i \neq j\,.
\end{equation}
Following the convention of mass normalisation, we scale the eigenvectors $\vec{\phi}_i$ such that the generalised mass for each mode is unity:
\begin{equation}
\label{massnormalisation}
\vec{\phi}_i^{\,\top} \!M \vec{\phi}_i = 1 \qquad \forall i\in\{1,\cdots,n\}\,.
\end{equation}
Consequently, we have the following relations between $\Phi$, $M$ and $K$:
\begin{equation}
\label{orthonormal}
\Phi^{\!\top} \!M \Phi = I\,, \qquad \Phi^{\!\top} \!K \Phi = \Omega^{2},
\end{equation}
where $I$ is the identity matrix and $\Omega = \text{diag}(\omega_1, \dots, \omega_n)$.
This allows us to perform a transformation $\vec{x}(t) = \Phi \vec{q}\hspace{0.1em}(t)$ into modal coordinates $\vec{q}\hspace{0.1em}(t)$ in which the mass and stiffness matrices are simultaneously diagonalised, decoupling the equations of motion. Substitution of $\vec{x}(t) = \Phi \vec{q}\hspace{0.1em}(t)$ in \autoref{ndogeq} yields:
\begin{equation}
M \Phi \ddot{\vec{q}} + C \Phi  \dot{\vec{q}} + K \Phi  \vec{q} = \vec{f}(t)\,.
\end{equation}
Left-multiplying by $\Phi^{\!\top}$ and using the relations in \autoref{orthonormal}, we obtain:\footnote{It is important to note that this decoupling is only possible if the system is classically damped, meaning that its damping matrix $C$ is also diagonalised by the undamped modal matrix $\Phi$.}
\begin{equation}
\label{transformation}
\ddot{\vec{q}} + 2 Z \Omega \dot{\vec{q}} + \Omega^{2} \vec{q} = \Phi^{\!\top} \!\vec{f}(t)\,,
\end{equation}
where $Z=\diag(\zeta_1, \cdots, \zeta_n)$ contains the modal damping ratios $\zeta_{i}$, which are related to the damping matrix $C$ via:
\begin{equation}
\zeta_i = \frac{\vec{\phi}_i^{\,\top} \!C  \vec{\phi}_i}{2 \, \omega_i}\,.
\end{equation}
We see from \autoref{transformation} that each modal coordinate $q_{i}(t)$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:modal-scalar}
\ddot{q}_{i}(t) + 2 \zeta_{i} \omega_{i} \dot{q}_{i}(t) + \omega_{i}^{2} q_{i}(t) = \vec{\phi}_{i}^{\,\top} \!\vec{f}(t)\,,
\end{equation}
which represents the decomposition into independent harmonic oscillators.

\section{Transfer functions}
In practice, we are often interested in the dynamical response of a system, which is characterised by transfer functions between scalar inputs and outputs. These functions are essential for performing frequency response and stability analyses. Suppose a scalar drive $u(t)$ is applied at a given degree of freedom or location represented by the input vector $\vec{b}$, so that:
\begin{equation}
\vec{f}(t) = \vec{b}  u(t)\,.
\end{equation}
Similarly, the output $y(t)$ is a scalar measurement of displacement:
\begin{equation}
y(t) = \vec{c}^{\top} \!\vec{x}(t) = \vec{c}^{\top} \!\Phi  \vec{q}\hspace{0.1em}(t)\,,
\end{equation}
where $\vec{c}$ is the output selection vector. For collocated driving and sensing, we have $\vec{b}=\vec{c}$. If we define the respective modal input and output coordinates as follows:
\begin{equation}
\vec{b}_{m} = \Phi^{\!\top} \vec{b}\,, \qquad \vec{c}_{m} = \Phi^{\!\top} \!\vec{c}\,,
\end{equation}
\autoref{eq:modal-scalar} can be expressed as:
\begin{equation}
\ddot{q}_{i}(t) + 2 \zeta_{i} \omega_{i} \dot{q}_{i}(t) + \omega_{i}^{2} q_{i}(t) = b_{m,i} \, u(t)\,,
\end{equation}
while the scalar output can be written as:
\begin{equation}
y(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} c_{m,i} \, q_{i}(t)\,.
\end{equation}
To compute the transfer function we take the Laplace transform under zero initial conditions. Let $Q_{i}(s)=\mathcal{L}\{q_{i}(t)\}$ and $U(s)=\mathcal{L}\{u(t)\}$. We can then write the modal equations as:
\begin{equation}
\bigl(s^{2} + 2 \zeta_{i} \omega_{i} s + \omega_{i}^{2}\bigr) Q_{i}(s) = b_{m,i} \, U(s)\,.
\end{equation}
The Laplace transform $Y(s)=\mathcal{L}\{y(t)\}$ of the output is therefore:
\begin{equation}
Y(s) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} c_{m,i} \, Q_{i}(s) 
= \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{c_{m,i} \,b_{m,i}}{s^{2} + 2 \zeta_{i} \omega_{i} s + \omega_{i}^{2}} \, U(s)\,.
\end{equation}
The transfer function $H(s)=Y(s)/U(s)$ can thus be written as:
\begin{equation}
H(s) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{\alpha_{i}}{s^{2} + 2 \zeta_{i} \omega_{i} s + \omega_{i}^{2}}\,,
\end{equation}
where we have defined the modal residues $\alpha_{i} = c_{m,i} \,b_{m,i}$, which quantify the participation of each mode in the chosen input-output pair. For collocated driving and sensing, we have $\alpha_{i}\geq 0\ \forall i\in\{1,\cdots,n\}$. Finally, setting $s=i\omega$ gives the frequency response function:
\begin{equation}
\label{freqtransferfunction}
H(s=i\omega) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{\alpha_{i}}{\omega_{i}^{2}-\omega^{2} + 2i \zeta_{i} \omega_{i} \omega}\,.
\end{equation}

\section{Resonances and anti-resonances}

\begin{equation}
H(i\omega) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{\alpha_{i}}{\omega_{i}^{2}-\omega^{2}}\,
\end{equation}
of an undamped system features $n$ pairs of complex conjugate poles and $n - 1$ pairs of complex conjugate zeros, such that between every two poles, there is a zero~\cite{Aphale2007}. \autoref{fig:tf_groupplot} shows the frequency response function of a two-degree-of-freedom mechanical system.

\section{Effective modal mass}
For a given mode vector $\phi_i$ and influence vector $\vec{r}$ which represents the displacements of the masses resulting from static application of a unit ground translation or rotation, the effective modal mass of mode $i$ is defined as:
\begin{equation}
m_i^{\mathrm{eff}}(\vec{r}\,) = \frac{\bigl(\vec{\phi}_i^{\,\top} \!M \vec{r}\,\bigr)^2}{\vec{\phi}_i^{\,\top} \!M \vec{\phi}_i}\,.
\end{equation}
Following the convention of mass-normalised mode vectors (\autoref{massnormalisation}), this expression reduces to:
\begin{equation}
m_i^{\mathrm{eff}}(\vec{r}\,) = \bigl(\vec{\phi}_i^{\,\top} \!M \vec{r}\,\bigr)^2 .
\end{equation}
The effective modal mass ratio or modal participation fraction $\mu_i(\vec{r}\,)$ of mode $i$ for the influence vector $\vec{r}$ is obtained by normalising the effective modal mass by the sum of all effective modal masses:
\begin{equation}
\label{modalmassratio}
\mu_i(\vec{r}\,) = \frac{m_i^{\mathrm{eff}}(\vec{r}\,)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i^{\mathrm{eff}}(\vec{r}\,)} = \frac{m_i^{\mathrm{eff}}(\vec{r}\,)}{\vec{r}^{\hspace{0.1em}\top}\!M\vec{r}}\,,
\end{equation}
such that the modal mass ratios satisfy $\sum_{i=1}^{n} \mu_i(\vec{r}\,)=1$. The last equality in \autoref{modalmassratio} follows from the fact that:
\begin{align}
\begin{split}
\sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i^{\mathrm{eff}}(\vec{r}\,)
&=\sum_{i=1}^{n} \bigl(\vec{\phi}_i^{\,\top}\!M\vec{r}\,\bigr)^2\\
&= \bigl(\Phi^{\!\top}\!M\vec{r}\,\bigr)^{\!\top}\bigl(\Phi^{\!\top}\!M\vec{r}\,\bigr)\\
&= \vec{r}^{\top} \!M \underbrace{\Phi\Phi^{\!\top} M}_{I} \vec{r}
= \vec{r}^{\top}\!M\vec{r}\,.
\end{split}
\end{align}
\autoref{modalmassratio} can be simplified further if $\vec{r}$ corresponds to unit translation of the entire structure in one Cartesian direction, in which case the effective modal masses for that translation sum to the total physical mass $M_\mathrm{tot}$ of the system: $\sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i^{\mathrm{eff}}(\vec{r}\,)=\vec{r}^{\top}\!M\vec{r}=M_{\mathrm{tot}}$.

\chapter{Stability Analysis of a Fibre-Tip Cavity Mount}
\label{app:legacymount}
This appendix presents a mechanical stability analysis of the legacy fibre-tip Fabry--Pérot resonator mount. The following three sections characterise this fibre-tip system through simulated modal frequency response, assessment of passive stability under environmental noise and measurement of the actuator transfer function. We report mode shapes, effective modal masses and measured spectra for frequencies below \SI{10}{\kilo\hertz}. The results provide a benchmark for direct comparison with the glass-substrate resonator mount introduced in \autoref{sec:mounting_structure}.

This distinction is practically relevant because the resonator fibres are mounted on either side of the access hole for the dipole-trap beam, where structural deformation directly alters the cavity length. The fundamental mode already affects the cavity length through this effect, but higher-order odd modes introduce stronger local curvature of the mounting arm at the fibre position, leading to much larger cavity-length fluctuations. Further insight is gained by comparing the effective modal mass across each translational and rotational degree of freedom (\autoref{modeparticipationM}). \section{Passive stability}
To evaluate the passive stability of the fibre-tip Fabry--Pérot resonator, we monitor its transmission under conditions such that the signal is directly proportional to cavity length. This is realised by operating the resonator in a low-finesse regime using \SI{1064}{\nano\meter} light, deliberately detuned from the mirror coating's design wavelength of \SI{780}{\nano\meter}. The lower reflectivity results in broader resonance profiles as shown in \autoref{fabryperot}. Before the transmission is recorded, the cavity length is adjusted with a piezoelectric actuator such that the transmitted optical power is at roughly \SI{50}{\percent} of its maximum value. At this point on the Airy distribution, the transmitted power varies approximately linearly with cavity length.

The measured signal reflects the product of the environmental vibration spectrum with the transfer function of the entire vacuum chamber and mounting assembly. The most prominent peak in the transmission spectrum appears at \SI{200}{\hertz}, which lies reasonably close to the first normal mode of the mounting structure at \SI{267}{\hertz} \textsc{(a)}. The discrepancy can be attributed to the additional mass of a small steel fibre holder that is fastened to the free end of the mounting structure with four screws (not shown in \autoref{marwanmodes}). When material is added at the free end of a cantilever, two competing effects arise: the added inertia lowers the resonant frequency, while the increased local stiffness raises it. For a local thickening near the free end of a cantilever, the inertial effect dominates, resulting a net downshift of the fundamental bending frequency. A second pronounced feature in the transmission spectrum is visible at \SI{1220}{\hertz}, closely matching modes \textsc{(b)} and \textsc{(c)} at \SI{1105}{\hertz} and \SI{1254}{\hertz}, respectively. This upward shift can be explained by the stiffening effect of the fibre support piece, which is more significant for these bending modes. In contrast, modes \textsc{(g)} and \textsc{(h)} are only weakly visible in the transmission spectrum, most likely because the environmental background provides limited excitation in this frequency range.

\section{Actuator transfer function}
In the previous section, the passive stability of the fibre-tip Fabry--Pérot resonator was characterised by analysing the transmission signal under ambient conditions. This approach, however, is inherently limited by the non-uniform power spectrum of the environmental noise. To obtain a more controlled and quantitative characterisation of the mechanical response, we measure the actuator transfer function of the fibre-tip resonator mount. For this purpose, one of the shear piezoelectric actuators that holds one end of the fibre-tip resonator is used to drive\footnote{Within the vacuum chamber, this configuration provides the only available means to actuate the mount mechanically.} the cavity and its mount sinusoidally, while the drive frequency is swept up to \SI{10}{\kilo\hertz} with an arbitrary waveform generator (AWG). The sinusoidal drive induces a corresponding oscillation of the cavity length and the response amplitude is expected to increase when the drive frequency coincides with a mechanical resonance of the mount. Strictly speaking, this measurement also probes resonances of the piezoelectric actuator itself, but these occur far above the frequency range of interest. The specified unloaded resonance frequency of the Noliac CSAP02 shear piezoelectric actuator is \SI{1.75}{\mega\hertz}. While the additional mass of the fibres and holders bonded to the actuator may reduce this resonance frequency, it is not expected to shift into the sub-\SI{10}{\kilo\hertz} range explored here. As in the passive stability analysis, the resonator is illuminated at \SI{1064}{\nano\meter} and a second piezoelectric actuator is used to stabilise the cavity length at the half-transmission point of a resonance slope, ensuring that the transmitted optical power remains approximately linear with respect to cavity length. The transmitted signal from the cavity is recorded on a photodiode and analysed with a spectrum analyser. By comparing the amplitude and phase of the photodiode signal to those of the AWG reference, we directly obtain the magnitude and phase of the transfer function $H(i\cdot 2\pi\nu)$, defined as the ratio of the cavity transmission response (photodiode output voltage) to the applied drive voltage before amplification. The results\footnote{The magnitude and phase of the transfer function were provided by Ezra Kassa.} are shown in \autoref{transferfunction}.

The measured transfer function of the fibre-tip resonator mount exhibits sharp resonance-anti-resonance pairs, each one of which qualitatively resembles the characteristic response of a two-mode mechanical system with collocated actuation and sensing (see \autoref{fig:tf_groupplot} in \autoref{app:modalanalysis}). In the present case, the shear piezoelectric actuator applies a local force along the $y$-axis of the mount at a position within \SI{5}{\milli\meter} of the cavity centre. The cavity transmission signal, which does not directly correspond to a measurement of displacement, rather responds to bending of the mount at this location, which results in the actuation and sensing points being nearly collocated.

If we examine the transfer function, the most prominent features appear close to the predicted resonances from the frequency response analysis for enforced acceleration of the base along the $y$-axis, where the visible modes associated with this direction of actuation are \textsc{(c)}, \textsc{(e)} and \textsc{(g)}. This behaviour is expected, since the shear piezoelectric actuator applies a harmonic force along the $y$-axis, though at a position near the free end of the cantilever. By contrast, the two lowest-frequency resonances that dominate the passive stability spectrum in \autoref{PSD} leave almost no signature in the transfer function, as they are primarily excited via enforced acceleration along the $z$- and $x$-axes, respectively (see \autoref{freqresponseMarw}). These resonances are thus excited more effectively by environmental vibrations that couple through the base in multiple directions. Interestingly, even though most of the other resonances \textsc{(a, b, d, f)} remain essentially invisible in the transfer function, we do observe mode \textsc{(h)} alongside mode \textsc{(g)}. In the simulations, however, mode \textsc{(h)} only appears for enforced acceleration along the $x$-axis. This suggests that, in practice, a small component of the actuator force may couple into the $x$-direction.

Finally, additional spectral features appear around \SI{9}{\kilo\hertz}. Since these do not emerge in the simulated modal response curves, they are most likely associated with the fibre-holder structure, which was not included in the finite-element model. These observations demonstrate that actuator transfer function measurements provide complementary information to the passive stability analysis. The latter reveals which modes are most strongly excited by ambient disturbances, while the former identifies which modes are efficiently driven and sensed in the actuator-cavity configuration. Taken together, these perspectives yield a more comprehensive picture of the dynamic behaviour of the fibre-tip resonator mount.
