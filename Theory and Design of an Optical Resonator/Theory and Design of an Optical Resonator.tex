\section{Introduction}
Single atoms are ideal candidates for implementing light-matter quantum interfaces, where the absorption or emission of a single photon can affect the optical response of the system. However, the atom-photon coupling strength in free space is limited by the small atomic cross section with respect to the spatial extent of the optical mode. A widely adopted strategy to overcome this problem is to place the emitter in an optical resonator to enhance its interaction strength with the field. This approach is described within the framework of cavity quantum electrodynamics (CQED), which captures the full quantum nature of these interactions, including phenomena such as vacuum Rabi oscillations and cavity-mediated population transfer (see \autoref{cqed}).

The central figure of merit in is the cooperativity $C$, which quantifies the ratio of coherent coupling to dissipative losses. Operating in the strong-coupling regime ($C\gg1$) is critical for the realisation of an efficient quantum interface between atoms and photons. This requires optical resonators with minimal loss and small mode volume. These criteria underpin the resonator design presented in this work.

Early demonstrations of strong coupling in CQED have been realised across a range of physical systems. Notable examples include atoms and ions in Fabry--Pérot resonators \cite{Thompson1992,Hiroki2020}, Rydberg atoms in microwave cavities~\cite{Brune1996}, superconducting qubits coupled to on-chip microwave resonators~\cite{Wallraff2004} or superconducting quantum interference devices (SQUIDs)~\cite{Chiorescu2004} and semiconductor quantum dots embedded in photonic crystals~\cite{Yoshie2004} or micropillar resonators~\cite{Reithmaier2004}.

A common limitation of many platforms is their poor compatibility with (near-infrared) optical photons, which are essential for high-fidelity, long-distance quantum communication over existing telecom fibre networks. Only ions and atoms possess optical transitions by nature and can therefore directly interface with optical photons when coupled to suitable resonators. Several architectures have been developed to achieve this, including Fabry--Pérot cavities~\cite{Thompson1992}, whispering-gallery-mode resonators---such as microspheres, microtoroids or bottle resonators~\cite{Vahala2003}---and photonic crystal cavities~\cite{Thompson2013}. Among these, Fabry--Pérot resonators were the first to facilitate strong coupling between light and alkali atoms in the optical domain \cite{Thompson1992}.

Experimental implementations of Fabry--Pérot resonators commonly achieve cavity linewidths on the order of a few \si{\mega\hertz}---well-matched to the natural linewidth of alkaline atomic transitions. These systems offer several advantages. Emitters within the cavity can be addressed transversely, decoupling resonator-mode interactions from auxiliary optical processes such as laser cooling, trapping, state preparation and driving V-STIRAP processes. The energy of photons inside the resonator is predominantly stored in the vacuum, which minimises losses from material absorption. Furthermore, neutral atoms remain isolated from dielectric surfaces, thereby preventing perturbative effects from surface charges and van der Waals forces. Piezoelectric elements are normally used for precise tunability and active stabilisation of the cavity length. Note that precise transverse alignment of the resonator mirrors is rarely required, since appropriately curved mirrors guarantee the existence of a stable mode. The Fabry--Pérot resonator forms the basis of the system investigated in this work and the following sections present a detailed account of its properties.

Light entering the resonator undergoes multiple reflections between the mirrors, causing constructive or destructive interference. This gives rise to resonator modes at certain frequencies and a characteristic lineshape of the transmitted light. The modal frequencies and lineshape will be discussed in the following sections.

\subsection{Resonance frequencies}
Whether the condition for constructive interference is satisfied depends on the round-trip phase accumulated by the light propagating inside the resonator. For light entering at a frequency of $\nu$, this round-trip phase shift (written as $2\phi$) equals twice the single-pass phase shift $\phi$, which can be expressed in terms of the wavenumber $k$ and the mirror separation $d$ as
\begin{align}
	\label{phiairy}
	\phi=kd=\pi\nu\frac{2d}{c}=\pi\frac{\nu}{\Delta\nu_\mathrm{FSR}}\,,
\end{align}
where $c$ denotes the speed of light in vacuum and $\Delta\nu_\mathrm{FSR}\coloneqq\sfrac{c}{2d}$, a quantity that is referred to as the free spectral range of the resonator. Resonances occur when light interferes constructively with itself upon a round-trip inside the cavity. This corresponds to frequencies for which the round-trip phase shift $2\phi$ equals a multiple of $2\pi$:
\begin{align}
	\label{rescondition}
	2\phi=2\pi q,\quad q \in \Z\,.
\end{align}
The integer $q$ is referred to as the mode index and labels the resonance that occurs at a frequency of $\nu_q=q\Delta\nu_\mathrm{FSR}$ or a wavenumber of $k_q=2\pi q \Delta\nu_\mathrm{FSR}/c$. It follows that the free spectral range $\Delta\nu_\mathrm{FSR}$ as defined earlier can be interpreted as the frequency spacing between two adjacent modes, as can be seen in \autoref{fabryperot}.

\subsection{Lineshape and finesse}
In this section, we derive the lineshape of the Fabry--Pérot resonator as shown in \autoref{fabryperot} by considering the transmitted field intensity ${\lvert E_\mathrm{t}\rvert^2}$ relative to the incident field intensity ${\lvert E_\mathrm{i}\rvert^2}$. This is done by relating both $ E_\mathrm{i}$ and $ E_\mathrm{t}$ to $ E_\mathrm{c}$, which is the steady-state amplitude of the field circulating inside the resonator. To find these relations, we assume that the optical thickness of the mirrors (lossless, thin dielectric slabs) is an odd number of quarter wavelengths. In addition, we consider the waves at separate reference planes that are located on opposite sides of this slab and are thus separated by the mirror thickness. In this case, the amplitude reflection coefficients from both sides of the mirror are symmetric and purely real (denoted $r_1$ and $r_2$). The amplitude transmission factors, however, acquire an additional phase shift of $\sfrac{\pi}{2}$ and can thus be written as $it_1$ and $it_2$, where $t_i\in \mathbb{R}$ and $r_i^2+t_i^2=1$ for $i\in \{ 1,2\}$. With these conventions, we can write
\begin{align}
	\begin{split}
		&E_\mathrm{t}= it_2 \mathrm{e}^{i\phi} E_\mathrm{c} \implies \frac{E_\mathrm{t}}{E_\mathrm{c}} = it_2 \mathrm{e}^{i\phi}\\
		&E_\mathrm{c}= it_1 E_\mathrm{i} + r_1 r_2 \mathrm{e}^{2i\phi} E_\mathrm{c} \implies \frac{E_\mathrm{c}}{E_\mathrm{i}} = \frac{it_1}{1-r_1 r_2 \mathrm{e}^{2i\phi}} \,.
	\end{split}
\end{align}
Using these results, we can calculate the normalised transmitted intensity:
\begin{align}
	\label{airy}
	\frac{\lvert E_\mathrm{t}\rvert^2}{\lvert E_\mathrm{i}\rvert^2} = \frac{\lvert -t_1 t_2 \mathrm{e}^{i\phi}\rvert^2}{\lvert 1-r_1 r_2\mathrm{e}^{2i\phi}\rvert^2}=\frac{(1-R_1)(1-R_2)}{\left(1-\sqrt{R_1R_2}\,\right)^2 + 4\sqrt{R_1R_2}\sin^2(\phi)}\,,
\end{align}
written in terms of the intensity reflectivities $R_1=r_1^2$ and $R_2=r_2^2$ of the left and right mirror, respectively. The form in \autoref{airy} represents the Airy distribution and is shown in \autoref{fabryperot} for different values of $R=R_1=R_2$.

A useful quantity in the context of optical resonators is finesse, which is a measure of how narrow the resonances are in relation to the free spectral range (higher finesse corresponding to narrower lines). The width $\Delta \nu_\mathrm{A}$ of a peak in the transmission profile of the resonator is defined as the full width at half maximum (FWHM), which can be derived from \autoref{airy} considering the phase change $\Delta \phi$ over which the Airy distribution drops from its maximum where $\sin^2(\phi)=0$ to half its maximum value:
\begin{align}
	\Delta\phi=\arcsin{\frac{1-\sqrt{R_1R_2}}{2\sqrt[4]{R_1R_2}}}\,.
\end{align}
Since $2\Delta\phi={\pi\Delta\nu_\mathrm{A}}/{\Delta\nu_\mathrm{FSR}}$ (see \autoref{phiairy}), we can calculate the finesse $\mathcal{F}$:
\begin{align}
	\mathcal{F}\coloneqq\frac{\Delta\nu_\mathrm{FSR}}{\Delta\nu_\mathrm{A}}=\frac{\pi}{2}\left(\arcsin{\frac{1-\sqrt{R_1R_2}}{2\sqrt[4]{R_1R_2}}}\right)^{-1}\,.
\end{align}
Note that the finesse is independent of the resonator length and is instead fully determined by the properties of the mirrors. For values of $\phi$ close to the nearest integer multiple of $\phi$, or more precisely $\min\{\lvert \phi \bmod \pi \rvert, \lvert \phi \bmod \pi - \pi\rvert\} \ll 1$, we can approximate $\sin^2(\phi)\approx\phi^2$ in \autoref{airy}, which yields
\begin{align}
	\label{approxfinesse}
	\mathcal{F}\approx \pi \frac{\sqrt[4]{R_1R_2}}{1-\sqrt{R_1R_2}}\,.
\end{align}
In the case of identical mirrors ($R_1=R_2=R$), \autoref{approxfinesse} reduces to
\begin{align}
\mathcal{F}\approx \pi\frac{\sqrt{R}}{1-R}\,.
\end{align}

\section{Transverse modes}
\label{sec:transversemodes}
In a Fabry--Pérot resonator, the standing wave patterns that form between the mirrors are not limited to a single uniform distribution across the transverse (perpendicular to the optical axis) plane but can exhibit various spatial intensity distributions, known as transverse modes. Each transverse mode corresponds to a specific solution of the paraxial Helmholtz equation within the resonator. The most fundamental modes are Gaussian, which have a single intensity peak centred on the optical axis. Higher-order transverse modes, such as Hermite--Gaussian, Laguerre--Gaussian, and Ince--Gaussian modes, exhibit increasingly complex intensity profiles with distinct nodal structures. These modes differ not only in their spatial energy distributions but also in their resonance frequencies due to their transverse confinement, a phenomenon known as transverse mode spacing. Throughout this section, we will discuss the paraxial Helmholtz equation that governs these solutions, which is followed by an analysis of Gaussian and higher-order modes.
\subsection{The paraxial Helmholtz equation}
The paraxial Helmholtz equation provides a fundamental framework for describing the propagation of light in optical systems where the beam divergence is small, and the paraxial approximation holds. Its solutions form the basis for understanding the transverse modes of Fabry--Pérot resonators, including Gaussian beams and their higher-order generalisations. The derivation and key properties of these solutions are presented in this section. We start from the Helmholtz equation, which reads
\begin{align}
	\label{Helmholtz}
	\left(\nabla^2 + k^2\right) E_0^{(+)}(\vec{r}\,)=0\,,
\end{align}
written in terms of the wavenumber $k$ and the complex electric field amplitude $E_0^{(+)}(\vec{r}\,)$ as defined in \autoref{Efield}. For a plane wave travelling along the $z$ direction, we can write the complex electric field amplitude as
\begin{align}
	E_0^{(+)}(\vec{r}\,)=u(\vec{r}\,)\mathrm{e}^{ikz}\,
\end{align}
where $u(\vec{r}\,)$ represents the complex transverse profile. In the paraxial approximation, this complex envelope varies sufficiently slowly with propagation distance $z$, such that the following inequalities are satisfied:
\begin{align}
	\left\lvert \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial z^2} \right\rvert \ll  \left\lvert 2k \frac{\partial u}{\partial z}\right\rvert , \left\lvert \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} \right\rvert, \left\lvert \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2} \right\rvert \,.
\end{align}
Under these conditions, \autoref{Helmholtz} simplifies to the paraxial Helmholtz equation, which is written as
\begin{align}
	\label{paraxialHelmholtz}
	\Delta_\perp u(\vec{r}\,)+2ik\frac{\partial}{\partial z}u(\vec{r}\,)=0\,,
\end{align}
where $\Delta_\perp={\partial^2}/{\partial x^2} + {\partial^2}/{\partial x^2}$ is the transverse Laplacian operator.
\subsection{Gaussian modes}
The Gaussian beam\footnote{With an intensity profile as given in \autoref{laser}.} is the fundamental solution to the paraxial Helmholtz equation and represents the simplest transverse mode of optical resonators with a spatially localised intensity profile. It can be written in cylindrical coordinates as~\cite{Saleh1991}
\begin{align}
	\label{gaussianmode}
	u(r, \phi, z) = \frac{w_0}{w(z)}\exp{\frac{-r^2}{w^2(z)}}\exp{i\left( kz + \frac{kr^2}{2R_\mathrm{c}(z)} - \Psi(z)\right)}\,,
\end{align}
in terms of the waist radius $w_0$, the beam radius $w(z)$ at a distance $z$ and Rayleigh length $z_\mathrm{R}$, which were given in \autoref{dipoleforce}. We have furthermore introduced the wavefront curvature radius $R_\mathrm{c}(z)=z(1+(\sfrac{z_\mathrm{R}}{z})^2)$ and the Guoy phase $\Psi(z)=\arctan\left(\sfrac{z}{z_\mathrm{R}}\right)$. The expression for $R_\mathrm{c}(z)$ illustrates how the wavefront curvature becomes spherical away from the beam waist and flattens as $z \to 0$. The Gouy phase accounts for the additional phase accumulated by a focused beam and increases smoothly from $\sfrac{-\pi}{2}$ to $\sfrac{\pi}{2}$ as the beam propagates through the focus. In the context of optical resonators, it is important to note that Gaussian beams constitute solutions to the paraxial Helmholtz equation subject to the boundary conditions imposed by a pair of spherical mirrors in a Fabry--Pérot cavity. This is due to the fact that a reflected Gaussian beam will retrace itself as long as the curvature of its wavefront matches that of the mirror. For a pair of concave, spherical mirrors separated by a distance $d$ and matching the Gaussian wavefront at their respective positions, the beam can, through repeated reflections, exist self-consistently within this optical resonator. Under the additional condition that the phase retraces itself upon reflection, the Gaussian beam is said to be a mode of the resonator. For a Gaussian mode in a symmetric spherical-mirror resonator (with mirror radii of curvature\footnote{A negative radius of curvature corresponds to a concave mirror. We therefore define the mirror radii of curvature as $R_{\mathrm{c},1}=R_\mathrm{c}(\sfrac{-d}{2})$ and $R_{\mathrm{c},2}=-R_\mathrm{c}(\sfrac{d}{2})$ in terms of the wavefront curvature radius $R_\mathrm{c}(z)$. For mirrors of equal curvature we write $R_{\mathrm{c},1}=R_{\mathrm{c},2}= R_\mathrm{c}$.} $R_{\mathrm{c},1}=R_{\mathrm{c},2}=-\lvert R_\mathrm{c} \rvert$ for concave mirrors), the beam waist $w_0$ and beam radii $w_1$ and $w_2$ at the mirror positions ($w_1=w(\sfrac{-d}{2})$ and $w_2=w(\sfrac{d}{2})$) can be calculated from the following relations:
\begin{align}
	\label{waisteqs}
	\begin{split}
		&w_0^2 = \frac{\lambda d}{2\pi}\sqrt{2\frac{\lvert R_\mathrm{c} \rvert}{d} - 1}\\
		&w_1^2 = w_2^2 = \frac{\lambda d}{\pi} \frac{1}{\sqrt{\sfrac{d}{\lvert R_\mathrm{c} \rvert}\left( 2 - \sfrac{d}{\lvert R_\mathrm{c} \rvert} \right)}}\,.
	\end{split}
\end{align}
To avoid unconfined solutions to the paraxial Helmholtz equation, which is to prevent diverging beam radii in \autoref{waisteqs}, the modulus of the mirror radius of curvature $\lvert R_\mathrm{c} \rvert$ and mirror separation $d$ must satisfy the confinement criterion:
\begin{align}
	0 \le \frac{d}{\lvert R_\mathrm{c} \rvert} \le 2\,.
\end{align}

\subsection{Higher-order modes}
\label{higerordermodesection}
Higher-order modes arise as alternative solutions to the paraxial Helmholtz equation, extending beyond the fundamental Gaussian mode. These modes retain the same underlying wavefront curvature and angular divergence as the Gaussian beam but exhibit more complex intensity distributions in the transverse plane. The higher-order transverse modes in a Fabry--Pérot resonator can be grouped into distinct families, each representing a complete set of exact, orthonormal solutions to the paraxial wave equation. This means that an arbitrary transverse field distribution can be decomposed into a unique combination of modes within a given family, the choice of which depends on the symmetry of the optical system. Hermite--Gaussian modes exhibit rectangular symmetry and as such, are expressed in Cartesian coordinates. In contrast, Laguerre--Gaussian modes feature radial symmetry and are defined in cylindrical coordinates. A third family of solutions, the Ince--Gaussian modes, have an inherent elliptical symmetry and describe an exact and continuous transition between Laguerre--Gaussian and Hermite--Gaussian solutions. We start with the general form of the normalised Hermite--Gaussian modes in Cartesian coordinates:
\begin{align}
\label{HG}
\begin{split}
&\mathcal{H}_{n_x,n_y}(x, y, z) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{ 2^{n_x+n_y} \pi n_x!n_y!}} \frac{1}{w(z)}H_{n_x}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}x}{w(z)}\right) H_{n_y}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}y}{w(z)}\right)\\ &\times\exp{-\frac{x^2 + y^2}{w^2(z)}} \exp{ i\left(kz + k\frac{x^2+y^2}{2R_\mathrm{c}(z)} - (n_x+n_y+1)\Psi(z))\right) } \,,
\end{split}
\end{align}
where $n_x$ and $n_y$ are non-negative integers representing the mode indices along the $x$- and $y$-axes, respectively. The functions $H_{n_x}$ and $H_{n_y}$ are the Hermite polynomials of order $n_x$ and $n_y$, which govern the transverse structure of the beam along the corresponding directions. Note the presence of the exponential factors from \autoref{gaussianmode}, which maintain the Gaussian envelope and wavefront curvature. Apart from the fundamental Gaussian mode $\mathcal{H}_{0,0}$, which is the only circularly symmetric member of the Hermite--Gaussian mode family, all higher order modes feature an enhanced Gouy phase shift due to the factor of $n_x+n_y+1$ in \autoref{HG}.

The normalised Laguerre--Gaussian modes can be written in cylindrical coordinates as
\begin{align}
\label{LG}
\begin{split}
&\mathcal{L}_{n,l}(r, \phi, z) = \sqrt{\frac{2n!}{\pi (n+\lvert l\rvert)!}}\frac{1}{w(z)} \left(\frac{\sqrt{2}r}{w(z)} \right)^{\!\vert l \rvert} L_n^{\lvert l \rvert} \left(\frac{2r^2}{w^2(z)}\right) \\ &\times\exp{\frac{-r^2}{w^2(z)}}\exp{ i\left(-l\phi + kz + \frac{kr^2}{2R_\mathrm{c}(z)} - (2n+\lvert l \rvert +1)\Psi(z)\right)} \,.
\end{split}
\end{align}
These modes are distinguished by a radial index $n$ and an azimuthal index $l$, the latter of which corresponds to the number of helical phase wraps around the beam axis. This is due to the factor of $\mathrm{e}^{ -il\phi}$, which gives rise to optical vortices and results in the beam carrying orbital angular momentum for $l\neq0$. The Laguerre polynomials $L_n^{\lvert l \rvert}(x)$, in conjunction with the factors of $x^{\vert l \rvert/2}$ and $\mathrm{e}^{x/2}$ (the Gaussian envelope) in \autoref{LG}, ensure the orthogonality of modes with equal azimuthal index. In addition, they are responsible for the characteristic radial intensity distribution associated with these modes.

The third family of complete orthogonal modes are the Ince--Gaussian modes, which are defined in an elliptic coordinate system and are subdivided into even and odd modes. Their respective expressions are
\begin{align}
\label{IG}
\begin{split}
&\mathcal{I}_{p,m}^{\mathrm{e}}(\vec{r}, \epsilon) = \frac{C w_0}{w(z)} C_p^m(i \xi, \epsilon) C_p^m(\eta, \epsilon)  \\
&\hspace{4em} \times \exp{\frac{-r^2}{w^2(z)}}\exp{i k z + \frac{k r^2}{2R_\mathrm{c}(z)} - (p + 1) \Psi(z)} \\ &\mathcal{I}_{p,m}^{\mathrm{o}}(\vec{r}, \epsilon) = \frac{S w_0}{w(z)} S_p^m(i \xi, \epsilon) S_p^m(\eta, \epsilon)  \\
&\hspace{4em} \times \exp{\frac{-r^2}{w^2(z)}}\exp{i k z + \frac{k r^2}{2R_\mathrm{c}(z)} - (p + 1) \Psi(z)}
\,,
\end{split}
\end{align}
in which $C$ and $S$ represent normalisation constants. $C_p^m$ and $S_p^m$ denote the even and odd Ince polynomials of order $p$ and degree $m$. The indices must be of equal parity ($(-1)^{p-m}=1$) and satisfy either $0 \leq m \leq p$ (even functions) or $1 \leq m \leq p$ (odd functions). The respective radial and angular elliptic coordinates $\xi\in \left[0,\infty \right)$ and $\eta\in\left[0,2\pi\right)$ are defined by
\begin{align}
&x = \sqrt{\frac{\epsilon}{2}} w(z) \cosh(\xi)\cos(\eta)\\
&y = \sqrt{\frac{\epsilon}{2}} w(z) \sinh(\xi)\sin(\eta)\,.
\end{align}
The parameter $\epsilon\in\left[0,\infty \right)$ represents the ellipticity of the transverse mode distribution. By continuously varying $\epsilon$, \autoref{IG} interpolates smoothly between Laguerre--Gaussian ($\epsilon\rightarrow 0$) and Hermite--Gaussian ($\epsilon\rightarrow \infty$) modes. This is shown for a number of Ince--Gaussian modes in \autoref{incegauss}.

\subsection{Transverse mode spacing}
For higher-order transverse modes, the resonance frequencies are shifted with respect to the fundamental mode. This shift depends on the Gouy phase, which is enhanced for higher-order modes (see \autoref{higerordermodesection}). Along the optical axis ($r=0$) of a Gaussian beam (\autoref{gaussianmode}), the resonance condition (\autoref{rescondition}) is still valid and the single-pass phase shift $\phi$ now equals
\begin{align}
\phi=kd-\Delta\Psi\,.
\end{align}
The phase retardation $\Delta\Psi$ relative to a plane wave is given by the difference in Gouy phase across the cavity:
\begin{align}
\label{deltapsi}
\Delta\Psi\coloneqq\Psi(\sfrac{d}{2})-\Psi(\sfrac{-d}{2})=2\arctan(\sfrac{d}{2z_\mathrm{R}})\,.
\end{align}
Using this result, it follows from \autoref{rescondition} that the resonance frequencies $\nu_q = \sfrac{k_qc}{2\pi}$ are given by
\begin{align}
\label{resfreqsG}
\nu_q = \left(q + \frac{\Delta\Psi}{\pi} \right)\Delta\nu_\mathrm{FSR}\,,
\end{align}
which yields the same mode spacing of $\Delta\nu_\mathrm{FSR}$ as obtained earlier. Following the same approach for the Hermite--Gaussian mode family, we obtain the following resonance frequencies:
\begin{align}
\label{resfreqsHG}
\nu_{q,n_x,n_y} = \left(q + \left(n_x+n_y+1\right)\frac{\Delta\Psi}{\pi} \right)\Delta\nu_\mathrm{FSR}\,,
\end{align}
which now depend on the longitudinal mode index $q$ and the transverse mode indices $n_x$ and $n_y$. Note that transverse modes are degenerate if their sum of the indices $n_x+n_y$ is the same. It is also worth noting that the mirror radius of curvature enters \autoref{resfreqsHG} via its effect on the Rayleigh length $z_\mathrm{R}$. In the limit of strongly curved, concentric mirrors ($\sfrac{d}{\lvert R_\mathrm{c} \rvert}\rightarrow 2$) the Rayleigh length vanishes (see \autoref{beamradiusincavity}), such that $\Delta\Psi\rightarrow\pi$, in which case the transverse and longitudinal modes become degenerate. A similar phenomenon occurs in confocal resonators ($\sfrac{d}{\lvert R_\mathrm{c} \rvert}= 1$), for which $z_\mathrm{R}=\sfrac{d}{2}$ and thus $\Delta\Psi=\sfrac{\pi}{2}$. In contrast to the previous case, this imposes the condition $n_x+n_y = 0 \pmod 2$ for degeneracy to occur.

The Fabry--Pérot resonator fabricated as part of this thesis is an asymmetric cavity consisting of one planar and one curved mirror. To allow meaningful comparison with standard symmetric resonator geometries such as confocal or plane-parallel cavities, we conceptually extend the cavity beyond the planar mirror by mirroring it, effectively forming a symmetric configuration of length $d = 2L$, where $L$ is the physical cavity length. In this extended picture, the relevant geometry parameter becomes $\sfrac{d}{\lvert R_\mathrm{c} \rvert} = 0.611$ (average\footnote{Since the fabricated mirrors are slightly elliptical, the mirror radii of curvature along the horizontal and vertical axes differ slightly. In this context, we consider the average of these two values.}), placing the design between a plane-parallel and a confocal configuration. This results in a tighter spacing of higher-order transverse modes, as can be observed from the simulated transverse mode spectrum in \autoref{transversemodespacing}.

In the context of coupling a single atom to a resonator mode, the fundamental Gaussian mode is often preferred due to its symmetric spatial profile and compatibility with single-mode optic fibres. In contrast, photons propagating through a multimode fibre suffer from mode mixing and modal interference, which distort the spatial coherence, especially over longer distances~\cite{Shekel2023}. This coherence is necessary to ensure photon indistinguishability for interference and entanglement protocols. A second complication with higher-order modes is their lack of intensity along the cavity axis, where atoms are usually trapped. Moreover, their larger spatial extent leads to greater losses per pass, as a larger proportion of optical power falls outside the finite mirror surfaces.

To isolate the Gaussian mode from higher-order transverse modes, the free spectral range and transverse mode spacing must not coincide. Since both parameters depend on the geometry of the cavity, certain configurations may result in the atom simultaneously coupling to multiple modes. However, a higher-order transverse mode exhibits a larger transverse mode area at the position of the atom, a quantity that is inversely proportional to the cooperativity of a Fabry--Pérot resonator~\cite{TanjiSuzuki2011}. This weakens the coupling strength between the atom and the (higher-order) mode. Consequently, in most practical applications, it is usually sufficient to focus on the positions of only a few of the lowest-order modes.

\section{Mirror design}
\label{mirrordesign}
Different mirror technologies for a Fabry--Pérot resonator have been employed in CQED experiments operating in the intermediate or strong coupling regime. These include dielectric coatings on superpolished macroscopic substrates~\cite{Casabone2013, Reimann2015, Welte2017}, as well as fibre-tip mirrors fabricated via mirror transfer~\cite{Steinmetz2006} or \ce{CO2} laser ablation~\cite{Hunger2010}. Fibre-tip cavities offer several advantages, including compactness, intrinsic fibre coupling and tight mode confinement enabled by the small achievable radii of curvature and short cavity lengths. These features allow for strong light-matter interaction and make such cavities promising candidates for integration into scalable quantum network architectures. However, they also suffer from several limitations, including mechanical fragility and alignment sensitivity. Furthermore, fibre-tip cavities suffer from a fundamental limitation in photon extraction efficiency as a result of the mismatch between the cavity mode and the guided mode of a single-mode fibre core~\cite{Gallego2016}. To overcome this, recent developments incorporate composite fibres with built-in mode-shaping structures~\cite{Gulati2017}, as well as photonic crystal fibres for improved mode matching~\cite{Ott2016}. Nonetheless, these approaches introduce fabrication complexity and still suffer from reduced mechanical robustness. In contrast, superpolished mirrors on macroscopic substrates offer excellent optical quality and mechanical stability, along with the flexibility to implement external mode-matching optics for efficient coupling to free-space or fibre-guided modes.

In our setup, shown in \autoref{resonatordesign}, a tapered, monolithic fused silica substrate is \ce{CO2} laser-ablated to produce a high-quality, strongly curved concave mirror surface on its polished end facet. Paired with a nearly planar mirror on a second, superpolished tapered substrate, this configuration achieves a small mode volume, enabling high coupling efficiency without sacrificing optical access or mechanical stability. The pyramidal substrate for the curved mirror is fabricated from UV-grade fused silica, selected for its low optical absorption and high transmission in the near-infrared. It features a square end facet of $\SI{275}{\micro\meter}\times\SI{275}{\micro\meter}$, polished with a fibre polishing tool (KrellTech SpecPro). A Gaussian-shaped depression is subsequently machined into the surface by laser ablation. The white-light interferogram in \autoref{resonatordesign} shows the resulting dimple profile, with an effective mirror diameter of approximately \SI{100}{\micro\meter}~\cite{doherty2021a}. The radii of curvature are $\lvert R_{\mathrm{c},x}\rvert = \SI{1.42}{\milli\meter}$ and $\lvert R_{\mathrm{c},y}\rvert = \SI{1.70}{\milli\meter}$, which indicate a slight ellipticity. A high-reflectivity dielectric coating is applied to the curved surface by Laseroptik GmbH and consists of alternating quarter-wave layers of \ce{Ta2O5} and \ce{SiO2} deposited via ion beam sputtering for a wavelength of $\lambda = \SI{780}{\nano\meter}$. The intensity transmission coefficient is $T = \SI{1.74}{ppm}$ and the total incoherent loss amounts to $\mathcal{L} = \SI{41.6}{ppm}$. The planar mirror is fabricated by REO (Part NS11334, Coating Run S7-254-P1/S7-251-P1) and features a high-transmission dielectric coating with $T = \SI{38.2}{ppm}$ and $\mathcal{L} = \SI{2.20}{ppm}$. The conical substrate is machined to a facet diameter of \SI{400}{\micro\meter} to match the pyramidal mirror. Using thermally curing epoxy (Epo-Tek 353ND), this substrate is mounted in a custom holder made from Macor. In contrast, the pyramidal mirror is self-supporting and does not require an additional mount. For any beam oriented perpendicular to the cavity axis, the angular aperture is determined by the substrate geometries and relative alignment. The half-angle angular aperture $\theta$ is defined as the half angle of the cone of light exiting the high-NA lens pupil and equals $\theta = \SI{40}{\degree}$ as shown in \autoref{resonatordesign}. This is sufficient to accommodate a numerical aperture of $\mathrm{NA} = 0.6$ (see \autoref{overviewsetup}), which corresponds to a half-angle of $\theta_{\mathrm{NA}} = \sin^{-1}(0.6) \approx \SI{36.9}{\degree}$.

In the first part of this section, the key components of this setup are described. The second part then outlines the procedure for precise placement of the resonator mirrors. During the alignment procedure, the element to be fixed (either a mirror substrate or the mode-matching lens) remains stationary in its clamp suspended from a gantry, while a reference beam for the cavity axis is moved into position using a U-bench mounted on a precision translation stage. This approach minimises vibration-induced misalignment during glueing, as it is mechanically more stable to translate the U-bench and all associated optics than to move a lightweight mirror clamp, which is more susceptible to mechanical vibrations. Kinematic fibre couplers (Thorlabs PAF-X-2-B) on either side of the U-bench (Thorlabs CBB1/M) are initially used to align the reference beam by optimising the coupling from one fibre into the other. Once the resonator mirrors have been placed within the cavity module, these couplers enable probe beam injection and detection of the transmission signal. A pair of lenses in translation mounts (Thorlabs CXY1A XY) ensure mode matching of this beam with the resonator mode. A \num{90}:\num{10} beam splitter, temporarily placed on the cavity module baseplate, allows side imaging of the cavity mode using a microscope (Navitar 1-50487).

The mirror substrates and mode-matching lens are held in bespoke PEEK clamps, individually machined for each component to ensure an optimal fit. Each clamp is equipped with a single nylon-tipped set screw to hold and release each component. The clamps can be coarsely positioned along the gantry rails and their height adjusted using a linear translation stage (Thorlabs MS1S/M), allowing retraction for epoxy injection before bonding. Once the pre-alignment step is complete, the mirror substrate remains fixed in its clamp while fine alignment is performed with a six-axis translation stage (Thorlabs MAX602D/M). This stage controls the position of the full U-bench along with the reference beam, the alignment mount and cavity module baseplate.

An aluminium mount is used to align the reference beam with respect to the cavity. This positions the cavity module in such a way that, once transferred into the vacuum chamber, the cavity axis runs centrally through the glass cell. The alignment mount also includes reference markers to aid in the precise positioning of the mirror substrate end facets. These markers consist of a horizontal pair of holes and a combination of a single hole and a machined dimple. Their intersection, highlighted by red dashed lines in \autoref{ubenchgantry}, defines the intended trapping region for atoms. During the alignment process, a second microscope is aligned along one of these lines to verify that the cavity mode coincides with it. This approach ensures full optical access for high-NA dipole trapping and efficient fluorescence collection by preventing clipping at the support cage apertures, which can occur if the cavity mode is not centrally aligned within the module.

As aforementioned, the first step involves aligning the probe beam along the central axis of the U-bench using the kinematic fibre couplers, a series of apertures mounted along the U-bench structure and a photodiode (Thorlabs PDA36A-EC) placed at the output. Additionally, a pair of lenses is introduced between the fibre couplers to mode-match the input beam to the expected cavity mode waist.

The second step is the installation of the planar mirror. Glued to its mount, the conical mirror substrate is lowered into the U-bench using the gantry system and held in place by a custom clamp. Its axial position is set using the reference markers in the alignment mount, as described earlier. The beam is visually centred on the mirror surface using the temporary beam splitter and primary microscope, after which the U-bench pitch and yaw are adjusted to achieve retro-reflection of the beam. This is verified by measuring the coupling back into the input fibre using a fibre-integrated beam splitter (Newport F-CPL-S22785-FCAPC) and photodiode. Once the mirror is correctly aligned, it is retracted vertically using a linear translation stage, epoxy is injected\footnote{The epoxy is applied sparingly and kept clear of the piezo edge to prevent the actuator from seizing.} onto the piezo and the mirror is lowered back into position. The epoxy (Norland Optical Adhesive 88) is cured under UV light for \SI{15}{\minute} before the clamp is released.

The third step concerns placement of the pyramidal mirror. It is introduced into the U-bench in the same way as the planar mirror. Its position along the cavity axis determines the resonator length and is set using a secondary microscope equipped with a calibrated measurement reticle. To facilitate cavity alignment, a \SI{635}{\nano\meter} beam is overlapped with the \SI{780}{\nano\meter} probe light at the fibre input. While the \SI{780}{\nano\meter} beam is used to probe the resonator mode, the \SI{635}{\nano\meter} light is transmitted in any case, making coarse alignment easier. Once transverse cavity modes are visible under the primary microscope, the U-bench pitch and yaw are adjusted to centre the mode on the curved mirror. The cavity length is then scanned using the piezo actuators to sweep over a free spectral range and the resulting transmission is monitored on an avalanche photodiode (Thorlabs APD110A/M). The pyramidal mirror is translated in the transverse plane to maximise coupling into the fundamental mode, as indicated by the photodiode signal. Once optimal coupling and mode placement are achieved, the mirror is secured with epoxy---again cured under UV light for \SI{15}{\minute}.

The final step is to install the integrated mode-matching lens. Before doing so, the cavity support cage is screwed onto the cavity module baseplate to stabilise the structure. The temporary output collimator lens in the U-bench is removed and the integrated plano-convex lens---pre-mounted in its custom holder---is introduced using the gantry and clamp system. Its position is fine-tuned by maximising the cavity transmission signal on the APD. Once this has been optimised, the lens holder is translated in its custom PEEK clamp and fixed in place using the same UV-curable epoxy (Norland Optical Adhesive 88).

\section{Characterisation}
\label{sec:characterisation}
In this section, we characterise the optical properties of the assembled Fabry--Pérot resonator. We begin by extracting the mode geometry from the frequency splitting between different modes, comparing the observed transmission spectrum to Hermite--Gaussian mode theory. From the transmission profile of a single resonance, we then determine the resonator linewidth, from which we infer the finesse and cavity decay rate. These results form the basis for estimating the atom-cavity coupling strength and cooperativity, which are the key figures of merit for CQED applications. Finally, we present a qualitative analysis of the transverse mode structure based on cavity transmission images, including higher-order Hermite--Gaussian and Laguerre--Gaussian modes.

\subsection{Coupling strength}
To evaluate the atom-cavity coupling strength $g_{\vec{k},\hat{\epsilon}}(\vec{r}\,)$ as defined in \autoref{couplingrate}, we require an explicit expression for the normalised field mode function $\vec{f}_{\vec{k},\hat{\epsilon}}(\vec{r}\,)$. For a standing-wave Gaussian mode confined between two ideal mirrors at $z = 0$ (planar) and $z = L$ (spherical), the scalar field envelope $u(r, z)$ takes the following form
\begin{equation}
u(r, z) = \frac{w_0}{w(z)} \exp\left( \frac{-r^2}{w^2(z)} \right) \sin(kz)\,,
\end{equation}
with wavenumber $k = q\pi / L$ for some longitudinal mode index $q$. The full (vector-valued) mode function is then given by
\begin{equation}
\vec{f}_{\vec{k},\hat{\epsilon}}(\vec{r}\,) = A\, u(r, z)\, \hat{\epsilon}\,,
\end{equation}
where $A$ is a normalisation constant to be determined. The separation into a scalar mode envelope $u(r, z)$ and a constant polarisation vector $\hat{\epsilon}$ is valid in the paraxial approximation, where the field predominantly propagates along the $z$-axis and the wavefront curvature is small. The normalisation condition for $\vec{f}_{\vec{k},\hat{\epsilon}}(\vec{r}\,)$, which ensures correct field quantisation, implies
\begin{equation}
\lvert A \rvert^2 \int \lvert u(r, z) \rvert^2\, \mathrm{d}^3 r = 1\,.
\end{equation}
Evaluating the integral using $\int_0^L \sin^2(kz)\, \mathrm{d}z = \sfrac{L}{2}$ for integer $q$ yields the normalisation constant
\begin{equation}
\lvert A \rvert = \sqrt{\frac{4}{\pi w_0^2 L}}\,.
\end{equation}
To estimate the maximum coupling strength inside the resonator, we examine the mode function, which reaches its maximum amplitude $\lvert \vec{f}_{\mathrm{max}} \rvert$ on the cavity axis ($r=0$) at the first antinode ($z = \sfrac{L}{2q}$, where $\sin(kz) = 1$): 
\begin{equation}
\lvert \vec{f}_{\mathrm{max}} \rvert = \lvert \vec{f}_{\vec{k},\hat{\epsilon}}(r=0, z=\sfrac{L}{2q}) \rvert = \lvert A \rvert \cdot \frac{w_0}{w(\sfrac{L}{2q})} \approx  \lvert A \rvert \,.
\end{equation}
The approximation holds when the first antinode lies well within the Rayleigh range: $\sfrac{L}{2q} \ll z_{\mathrm{R}}$. In our resonator, $L=\SI{476}{\micro\meter}$ and $q\sim 10^3$, which yield $\sfrac{L}{2q}\sim \SI{0.2}{\micro\meter} \ll \SI{718}{\micro\meter} = z_\mathrm{R}$.
Since $L \ll z_\mathrm{R}$, the approximation $w(z) \approx w_0$ holds across the entire cavity length. Thus, the coupling strength is maximised regardless of longitudinal position, provided the atom is placed at an antinode and the polarisation $\hat{\epsilon}$ is aligned with the transition dipole moment $\langle \mathrm{e} \vert \hat{\vec{d}}\, \vert \mathrm{g} \rangle$. Under these conditions, the maximally achievable single-photon coupling strength $g_{\mathrm{max}}$ can be written as
\begin{equation}
g_{\mathrm{max}} \approx \lvert A \rvert \sqrt{\frac{\omega}{2\epsilon_0 \hbar}} \, \lvert \langle \mathrm{e} \vert \hat{\vec{d}}\, \vert \mathrm{g} \rangle \rvert =  \sqrt{\frac{2\omega}{\pi\epsilon_0 \hbar w_0^2 L}} \, \lvert \langle \mathrm{e} \vert \hat{\vec{d}}\, \vert \mathrm{g} \rangle \rvert \,.
\label{eq:gmax}
\end{equation}
To estimate the value of $g_\mathrm{max}$, we must consider a suitable choice for $\vert \mathrm{g} \rangle$ and $\vert \mathrm{e} \rangle$ in the context of cavity-mediated population transfer for the extraction and shaping of single photons from  \ce{^87Rb} atoms (see \autoref{vstirap}). The V-STIRAP protocol can be executed in a $\Lambda$-like three level system (\autoref{fig:3lvlLambdaSystem}) with, for instance, $\vert \mathrm{g} \rangle= \lvert F = 1, m_F = -1 \rangle$, $ \lvert \mathrm{e} \rangle = \lvert F' = 1, m_F' = 0 \rangle $ and $\lvert \mathrm{u} \rangle = \lvert F=2, m_F = 0 \rangle $. Considering the relevant Clebsch--Gordan coefficient, we thus obtain
\begin{equation}
 \lvert \langle \mathrm{e} \vert \hat{\vec{d}}\, \vert \mathrm{g} \rangle \rvert = \sqrt{\sfrac{5}{24}} \times \lvert \langle J = \sfrac{1}{2} \| \hat{\vec{d}}\, \| J' = \sfrac{3}{2} \rangle \rvert= \SI{1.636e-29}{\coulomb\meter}\,.
\end{equation}
With a mode waist $ w_0 = \SI{13.3}{\micro\meter} $ and resonator length $ L = \SI{476}{\micro\meter}$, \autoref{eq:gmax} yields a maximum atom-cavity coupling strength of
\begin{equation}
g_{\mathrm{max}} \approx 2\pi\times\SI{11.5}{\mega\hertz}\,.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Cooperativity}
In the preceding two sections, we have estimated the cavity field decay rate $\kappa$ from the measured linewidth and extracted the (maximum) atom-cavity coupling strength $g_\mathrm{max}$ from the cavity mode geometry. We now complete the set of key parameters with the transverse polarisation decay rate $\gamma= 2\pi \times \SI{3.03}{\mega\hertz}$ for the $\mathrm{D}2$ transition in \ce{^87Rb}, which yields a cooperativity of $C=\num{10.4}$ (see \autoref{cooperativity}). The measured profiles are more accurately described by even Ince--Gaussian modes with finite ellipticity $\epsilon < \infty$, rather than by Hermite--Gaussian modes with $\varepsilon \to \infty$. Regarding the Laguerre--Gaussian modes, the characteristic ring-like intensity profile of the $\mathcal{L}_{1,0}$ mode in \autoref{cavitymodes} arises from the underlying phase, which advances continuously as a function of the azimuthal coordinate, forming a $2\pi$ vortex as seen in \autoref{cavitymodestheory}. The destructive interference at the centre caused by this phase singularity leads to the observed intensity dip.

\section{Conclusion}
In this chapter, we have presented the theoretical foundations and practical realisation of a Fabry--Pérot resonator designed as a platform for interfacing trapped neutral atoms with single photons. The cavity comprises a high-transmission ($T = \SI{38.2}{ppm}$) super-polished near-planar mirror serving as the photonic outcoupling port, paired with a low-transmission ($T = \SI{1.74}{ppm}$) strongly curved micro-mirror, ablated onto a pyramidal substrate to achieve strong optical confinement. The resulting high-finesse resonator ($\mathcal{F} = \num{75.1 \pm 0.9e3}$) exhibits a narrow linewidth ($\Delta\nu_\mathrm{A} = \SI{4.20}{\mega\hertz}$) well matched to the hyperfine transitions of the $\mathrm{D}2$ line in \ce{^{87}Rb}. The key CQED parameters are found to be $(g_\mathrm{max}, \gamma, \kappa) = 2\pi \times (11.5, 3.03, 2.10)\,\si{\mega\hertz}$, yielding a cooperativity of $C = \num{10.4}$. These promising parameters place the system in the strong coupling regime, as required for single-photon generation based on cavity-mediated population transfer protocols.

The cavity design features a half-angle angular aperture of $\theta = \SI{40}{\degree}$, sufficient to accommodate a high-NA (\num{0.6}) optical dipole trap for atom confinement within the resonator, the implementation of which is deferred to future work. The modular architecture of the cavity support structure nonetheless enables integration with both magneto-optical and dipole trapping setups, which will be discussed in the following chapter (\autoref{chap:CavityCompatibleTrappingArchitectures}). Following a similar design to that presented in this chapter, simultaneous operation of three cavities using a single pair of substrates has been demonstrated \cite{Doherty2023}. Frequency locking, however, remains a challenge that is not addressed in this work.
