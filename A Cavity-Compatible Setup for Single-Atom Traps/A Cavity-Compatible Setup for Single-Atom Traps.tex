In this chapter, we present a mechanical assembly comprising a high-finesse optical resonator, a tightly focused optical dipole trap and a compact cold-atom source based on a pyramidal magneto-optical trap (MOT). Following an introduction that sets the broader context for cavity-compatible atom trapping, we discuss our arrangement in \autoref{sec:mounting_structure}, including a detailed mechanical stability and vibration analysis. In \autoref{sec:pyramidal_mot}, we describe the pyramidal MOT, starting from its basic operating principle and continuing with an analysis of aperture diffraction and methods used for cold-atom detection. The chapter closes with a summary of the main design choices and implications for future work on intra-cavity single-atom trapping.

\section{Introduction}
Efficient atom-photon interfaces are key to quantum technology applications, allowing for deterministic single-photon generation and the distribution of remote entanglement. As discussed in \autoref{chap:TheoryandDesignofanOpticalResonator}, the atom-photon coupling can be strengthened significantly by placing the emitter in a high-finesse optical cavity. This supports deterministic emission into one mode of the radiation field for the delivery of single photons. This has been demonstrated using an atomic fountain that loads \ce{^87Rb} atoms stochastically into a cavity~\cite{Barrett2019}. In this case, the loading efficiency is kept low to suppress the likelihood of multi-atom occupancy within the resonator. This limits the interaction time and reduces the duty cycle. The transient nature of atomic loading gives furthermore rise to a time-dependent coupling strength, reducing the efficiency of V-STIRAP. Stochastic loading becomes ineffective when multiple cavities must be loaded simultaneously---a requirement for realising entanglement protocols based on the interference of indistinguishable photons emitted from separate atom-cavity systems (see \autoref{app:cavityentanglement} in \autoref{CavityMediatedEntanglement}). This follows from the fact that the likelihood of loading atoms into two different cavities is comparable to the likelihood of loading two atoms into the same cavity, which is undesirable (see \autoref{app:stochasticcavityloading} in \autoref{CavityMediatedEntanglement}).

While such platforms constitute excellent test beds, probabilistic loading fundamentally limits scalability. To maximise the single-atom occupation probability of an optical resonator, an atom can be confined in a tightly focused optical dipole trap operating in the collisional blockade regime (see \autoref{collisionalblockade}). By employing holographic optical tweezer arrays, multiple atoms can be positioned with high precision within the cavity mode, enabling simultaneous coupling of several emitters. Alternatively, such arrays can act as a quantum register, where individual atoms are stored outside the mode volume and dynamically shuttled in and out to interact with the cavity field as required.

One challenge with this approach, however, is that direct loading of the dipole traps from a MOT is often impractical due to the restricted optical access for the MOT beams in the space between the cavity mirrors. Instead, the MOT cloud must be positioned in the vicinity of the cavity, from which atoms are subsequently transported into the trapping region inside the resonator. A number of such approaches have been implemented, including funnelling atoms from an atomic fountain into the cavity using blue-detuned cavity modes~\cite{Puppe2007}, guiding atoms from a static MOT with a single red-detuned beam~\cite{Numann2005} and employing an optical lattice as a conveyor belt for atom transport~\cite{Gallego2018,Schrader2001}.

Here, our approach for a cavity-compatible cold-atom source is based on the pyramidal-MOT concept first introduced in 1996~\cite{Lee1996}. In this geometry, four reflective facets form a hollow pyramid that redirects a single incident cooling beam to create a compact three-dimensional trap. When configured with a small aperture at the apex, this setup can produce a continuous beam of cold atoms emanating along the pyramid axis~\cite{Arlt1998,Williamson1998}. When directed towards the trapping region between the cavity mirrors, the atomic beam serves to load the intra-cavity dipole traps. In the sections that follow, we examine the design choices and considerations underpinning a stable and modular apparatus that integrates the optical resonator presented in \autoref{chap:TheoryandDesignofanOpticalResonator} with a pyramidal-MOT cold-atom source and single-atom dipole traps.

\section{Design principles for resonator stability}
\label{subsec:mechanicalstab}
Fabry--Pérot resonators underpin a wide range of applications, from cavity optomechanical sensing~\cite{Aspelmeyer2014} and gravitational-wave detection~\cite{Abbott2016} to optical clocks~\cite{Hinkley2013} and CQED experiments~\cite{Mabuchi2002,Birnbaum2005,Steiner2013}. In all these contexts, active stabilisation of the cavity length is essential to suppress environmental fluctuations and maintain resonance. This is achieved by locking to an external frequency reference via feedback to the actuators that control the mirror positions. The usable feedback bandwidth is ultimately constrained by the mechanical response of the resonator assembly, since sharp vibrational resonances in the mirrors or their mounts directly affect the control loop and reduce stability.

A variety of approaches have been explored to improve the mechanical stability of Fabry--Pérot cavities, especially in the fields of laser frequency stabilisation and interferometry~\cite{Notcutt2005,Briles2010,Sinclair2015}. For CQED experiments, however, the constraints of compact UHV environments limit the direct use of such damping or mounting strategies. Active suppression methods, for instance employing digital filters to cancel mechanical resonances within feedback loops~\cite{Ryou2017}, can provide improvements, but add considerable complexity to the stabilisation system.

A more robust solution is to design the cavity assembly for passive stability from the outset, reducing the presence of low-frequency resonances that would otherwise need to be actively compensated. Previous work has shown that resonances can be shifted to higher frequencies and suppressed in quality factor by maximising stiffness and introducing damping in the mounting structure~\cite{Briles2010, Sinclair2015}. For the glass-substrate Fabry--Pérot resonator introduced in \autoref{chap:TheoryandDesignofanOpticalResonator}, we pursue passive stability by maximising the steel mass that can be accommodated within the compact glass cell and by introducing geometric irregularities in the mount to avoid degenerate mechanical modes. Breaking symmetry reduces peak gain in the response function by separating modes and limiting energy transfer between axes.

\section{Integrated design and stability analysis}
\label{sec:mounting_structure}
The central component of the cavity-compatible trapping setup is a rigid mounting structure which secures the cavity module whilst accommodating the in-vacuum optics for the pyramidal-MOT source. This section presents the assembly layout and evaluates its stability through a mechanical frequency response analysis, with a direct comparison to a legacy fibre-tip resonator mount.

\subsection{Overview}
\label{subsec:mountingoverview}
Following the principles outlined in \autoref{subsec:mechanicalstab}, the mount is designed for passive stability by maximising the steel mass inside the glass cell and by shaping the structure to break degeneracies in the mechanical response spectrum. This design philosophy aims to shift resonances out of the critical low-frequency regime and provide a more robust platform for stabilising cavity length.

The mounting structure, shown in \autoref{fig:fingeroverview}, is machined from 316LN stainless steel and houses the cavity module (see \autoref{cavitymodulesection}), which slides in through the central square aperture and is fixed at the correct position with six countersunk screws. In addition, the mounting structure hosts a compact pyramidal mirror assembly (see \autoref{sec:pyramidal_mot}), held in place by a custom aluminium holder inserted from the side.

\subsection{Modal frequency response analysis}
\label{glasssubstratemodalfreqresponse}
Modal frequency response analysis is a computational technique used to predict how a structure responds dynamically to base excitations across a range of frequencies (see \autoref{app:modalanalysis}). By decomposing the system into its eigenmodes, each characterised by a natural frequency and mode shape, the method allows us to evaluate the contribution of individual modes to the overall dynamic behaviour. This approach is particularly effective for multi-degree-of-freedom (MDOF) systems, where the number of vibrational modes can be large, but only a subset significantly influences the response under typical operating conditions.

The modal frequency response of the glass-substrate resonator mount (including cavity module) is evaluated through finite-element modelling, implemented in Autodesk Inventor using the Nastran solver. The analysis uses \num{8} processor threads\footnote{Using around \num{8} threads is near optimal for this analysis. Using more threads increases overhead from distributing the calculation and does not necessarily reduce total runtime.} on an AMD Ryzen Threadripper 3990X 64-Core Processor (\SI{2.90}{\giga\hertz}) with \SI{256}{\giga\byte} of RAM. A parabolic mesh with an element size of \SI{1}{\milli\metre} is used, resulting in a total of \num{330715} elements. To capture the dominant dynamic behaviour, \num{80} modes are retained in the analysis, such that the cumulative effective modal mass reaches at least \SI{80}{\percent} of the total physical mass for each degree of freedom. The effective modal mass ratios are listed in \autoref{modeparticipationM} (see \autoref{app:effectivemodalmass}). Base excitations with an acceleration magnitude of \SI{1}{\milli\metre\per\second\squared} are enforced individually in the $x$, $y$ and $z$ directions at the mounting interface to the vacuum chamber flange. The frequency response is evaluated between \SI{0}{\kilo\hertz} and \SI{10}{\kilo\hertz} with \num{15} data points between each pair of successive modes and a bias factor of \num{3} to ensure denser sampling near the resonance frequencies. The maximum linear acceleration magnitudes of the structure for these three excitation directions are presented in \autoref{freqresponseFF}.

\subsection{Comparison with a legacy resonator mount}

introduce and refer to legacy design in \autoref{app:legacymount}

The fibre-tip resonator mount, while simple in its design, suffers from poor mechanical stability. A dense set of low-frequency resonances spread across the simulated frequency response and measured transfer function makes the cavity highly sensitive to environmental vibrations and difficult to operate reliably. In particular, the presence of strong resonances complicates active feedback stabilisation of cavity length, since the controller gain must be reduced around these resonances to maintain stability, which in turn limits the achievable feedback bandwidth. 

The first observation is that the glass-substrate resonator mount exhibits fewer resonance peaks, especially within the \SIrange{0}{5}{\kilo\hertz} range, where we previously observed six modes. The lowest frequency mode for the glass-substrate resonator mount is located at $\SI{1.89}{\kilo\hertz}$ \textsc{(a)}, which is in sharp contrast to the fundamental bending mode at $\SI{267}{\hertz}$ for the fibre-tip resonator mount. Pushing the fundamental mode upward by a factor of seven reduces the structure's vulnerability to low-frequency ambient excitation. The proximity of the second mode at $\SI{1.98}{\kilo\hertz}$ to the first reflects the transition from the highly asymmetric rectangular cross-section to the more symmetric square cross-section of the current design, which is an unavoidable consequence of maximising the available steel volume within the (cuboid) glass cell. When comparing the effective modal mass ratios in \autoref{modeparticipationM} with those in \autoref{tab:modepercentFinalfinger}, we observe that the lowest two modes for the earlier design show a strong participation in the rotational degrees of freedom, with effective mass ratios of $\SI{85.1}{\percent}$ for $R_x$ (first mode) and $\SI{83.5}{\percent}$ for $R_z$ (second mode). In contrast, the glass-substrate resonator mount exhibits strong translational participation for the first two modes, with effective mass ratios of $\SI{35.8}{\percent}$ for $T_z$ (first mode) and $\SI{35.7}{\percent}$ for $T_x$ (second mode). This discrepancy arises because the fibre-tip resonator mount features a smaller support at the base (compare \autoref{marwanmodes} with \autoref{FFmodes}), so the cantilever deforms by bending and rotating about an axis close to the origin. By contrast, the glass-substrate resonator mount consists of a tapered support that extends over nearly half the structure. As a result, the fundamental mode no longer pivots about an axis near the origin but instead buckles at the interface with the cuboid section of the mount, resulting in stronger modal mass participation for the translational degree of freedom.

To complement the previous analysis of structural acceleration, it is useful to consider the displacement response, since the cavity length is directly affected by deformations of the mount. This provides a more accurate representation of which modes are most detrimental to the stability of the resonator. Prominent acceleration peaks at high frequency may correspond to negligible displacements, in contrast to low-frequency modes. For a harmonic base acceleration $\vec{a}(t) = \vec{a}_0 \cos(\omega t)$, the corresponding rigid-body displacement amplitude scales as $\lvert \vec{x}(t) \rvert = \lvert \vec{a}_0 \rvert / \omega^2$. Thus, away from resonances, the displacement response decreases as $\sfrac{1}{\omega^2}$. 

The curves in \autoref{freqresponsecomparison} show the maximum displacement magnitude for both mounting designs under enforced base acceleration of $\lvert \vec{a}_0 \rvert = \SI{1}{\milli\metre\per\second\squared}$.

\section{Pyramidal magneto-optical trap}
\label{sec:pyramidal_mot}
In this section, we discuss a pyramidal magneto-optical trap as a compact, cavity-compatible cold-atom source that can deliver atoms into the region between the cavity mirrors to load single-atom dipole traps. In \autoref{pyramidMOToverview} we describe the operating principle of the pyramidal MOT and its implementation in our setup. An important consideration is that the aperture at the pyramid apex, which allows atoms to pass towards the cavity centre, inevitably alters the reflected cooling beams through diffraction. The impact of this effect is examined in \autoref{pyramidMOTaperture}. The fabrication of the mirror substrates and their assembly into the pyramidal configuration is detailed in \autoref{pyramidMOTmirrorfab}. Finally, \autoref{pyramidMOTcoldatom} outlines the detection of cold atoms trapped with the assembled pyramidal MOT in a stand-alone test configuration.

\subsection{Overview}
\label{pyramidMOToverview}
The choice of a pyramidal MOT as the cold-atom source is motivated by its compactness, mechanical robustness and minimal optical access requirements. Conventional MOTs use three orthogonal pairs of counter-propagating laser beams, requiring multiple independent optical elements and substantial optical access to the trapping region. While retro-reflection can halve the number of independent beams, the apparatus remains relatively bulky and alignment-sensitive. In contrast, the pyramidal MOT uses a set of four right-angle mirrors in a square-based\footnote{Alternative mirror arrangements have also been demonstrated~\cite{Vangeleyn2009, Kim1997, Vangeleyn2010}.} pyramidal configuration to reflect a circularly polarised input beam into the full six-beam arrangement required for magneto-optical trapping in a spherical quadrupole magnetic field. Each pair of counter-propagating beams emerges with the correct relative circular polarisation by virtue of the \SI{180}{\degree} phase shift between the $s$- and $p$-components of the light upon reflection from the mirrors. This geometry, requiring only a single incident beam, yields balanced intensities at all points in the trapping volume (assuming well-aligned and identical mirrors), making efficient use of the available optical power.

By truncating the pyramid apex to form an aperture, the configuration can be operated as a source, where atoms are extracted along the symmetry axis to load a downstream trap---in our case, the optical dipole trap inside the cavity mode as illustrated in \autoref{pyramidloading}.

Such pyramidal-MOT sources have been demonstrated both as pulsed emitters of cold atoms~\cite{Arlt1998} and as continuous funnels capable of efficiently transferring atoms into a secondary MOT~\cite{Williamson1998}. The ejection is a direct result of asymmetric radiation pressure. The pyramidal mirrors provide cooling and transverse confinement by creating a balanced scattering force from all sides. In the central region in front of the aperture, however, the counter-propagating beam is absent. Atoms in this volume scatter only from the unopposed input beam, resulting in a net force that accelerates them through the hole. Acceleration continues until the atoms are Doppler shifted out of resonance, suppressing further scattering. This produces a slow atomic beam with a velocity matched to the capture range of a downstream MOT or optical molasses.

In order to load atoms from the pyramidal MOT into the intra-cavity dipole trap, a local optical molasses is required. This is realised by directing two cooling beams onto the trapping region as illustrated in \autoref{pyramidloading}. Reflections from the gold-coated mirror holder provide the necessary counter-propagating components, establishing two (non-orthogonal) beam pairs in this volume to form a molasses that captures and cools the atoms emerging from the pyramidal MOT.

\subsection{Aperture diffraction analysis}
\label{pyramidMOTaperture}
A simple ray-optics picture suggests that any finite square aperture at the apex of a pyramidal MOT would provide a cuboid channel through which atoms can escape, since the retro-reflected light is absent in this region. In practice, however, diffraction of the retro-reflected beams around the aperture leads to a residual intensity that reduces the axial scattering force imbalance, yielding a slower output beam~\cite{ravenhall2018a}. While this can help increase the capture probability in a downstream MOT or optical molasses, the primary risk of diffraction is that it effectively narrows the exit channel. This diffractive narrowing suppresses the atomic flux by increasing the probability of collisional loss. Atoms with large transverse velocities are more likely to leave the exit channel before arriving at the aperture. This increases the average number of recycling events that an atom undergoes, which prolongs the time $\tau$ for an atom to be cooled and reach the exit. The probability that an atom is lost due to background vapour collisions during this time is
\begin{equation}
P_{\rm loss} = 1 - \mathrm{e}^{-\gamma_1 \tau}\,,
\end{equation}
where $\gamma_1$ is the single-body collisional loss rate.\footnote{Here, $\gamma_1 = n \sigma_{\rm c} \bar{v}$, with $n$ the number density of the background gas, $\sigma_{\rm c}$ the collisional cross-section and $\bar{v}$ the mean speed of background atoms. The formula follows from a Poissonian treatment of collisions: $\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma_1 \tau}$ is the probability that no collision occurs in time $\tau$, so $1 - \mathrm{e}^{-\gamma_1 \tau}$ gives the probability of at least one collision, resulting in loss.} Consequently, the longer dwell time $\tau$ in a narrow-aperture pyramidal MOT directly increases the loss probability via collisions, thereby reducing the atomic flux.

In a recent study of a compact pyramidal MOT, square apertures with side lengths below $\SI{0.3}{\milli\meter}$ resulted in a negligible flux, which increased roughly linearly up to an aperture size of $\SI{1}{\milli\meter}$ before showing signs of saturation~\cite{Ravenhall2021}. Although increasing the aperture size can, in principle, enhance the atomic flux, this does not necessarily apply for a small pyramidal MOT. Our system is considerably smaller than typical high-flux sources, with an expected MOT diameter of only a few millimetres. In this regime, an aperture on the order of $\SI{1}{\milli\meter}$ would severely limit the effective trapping volume and hence reduce the capture rate of the MOT. We therefore aim for a sub-millimetre aperture that balances competing requirements. It must be small enough to maintain a sufficiently large MOT capture volume, yet not so small that diffraction narrows the exit channel and consequently suppresses the atomic flux of the cold atom source.

To quantify this latter effect, we perform a near-field diffraction analysis. As illustrated in \autoref{motdiffraction}, the reflected beams can be modelled as an incident plane wave diffracting from a square obstruction.

The diffraction patterns are calculated numerically on a $2048\times 2048$ grid using a standard FFT-based propagation method within the Fresnel approximation, which assumes paraxial propagation and neglects higher-order phase terms. The validity of the Fresnel approximation for near-field diffraction can be assessed via the Fresnel number:
\begin{equation}
N_\mathrm{F} = \frac{a^{2}}{4 \lambda d}\,,
\end{equation}
in terms of the wavelength $\lambda$ and the characteristic dimensions shown in \autoref{motdiffraction}. For $N_\mathrm{F} \gtrsim 1$, the system is in the Fresnel regime, where diffraction fringes are strongly influenced by near-field effects, whereas $N_\mathrm{F} \ll 1$ corresponds to the Fraunhofer (far-field) limit.

In particular, the region directly in front of the aperture is accurate to within a few per cent. Increased errors also appear near the domain boundaries, with the direct Fresnel--Kirchhoff integration resulting in noticeable artefacts. Compared with the FFT-based Fresnel propagation, direct evaluation of the Fresnel--Kirchhoff integral is more sensitive to the finite size of the computational window. Although the integral extends over the entire transverse plane, we evaluate it on a finite grid, which is equivalent to applying a top-hat window to the input field. This artificial aperture introduces diffraction that can propagate into the region of interest. By contrast, the FFT approach enforces periodic boundary conditions on the field, which avoids the hard step to zero that would otherwise behave like a diffracting edge. Because the FFT imposes periodic boundaries, any non-negligible field at the edges is recycled from the opposite side, so periodic replicas contaminate the solution even if no fringes are visible. This contamination grows with propagation distance $d$, as the Fresnel kernel spreads and couples more power from the periodic images into the region of interest. This underlines the need for a simulation domain that is significantly larger than the region of interest. \autoref{differror} and \autoref{differrorcross} together demonstrate that restricting the computation to an area of $\SI{1}{\milli\metre}\times\SI{1}{\milli\metre}$ leads to gross errors across the entire domain, with only the aperture region featuring errors below \SI{10}{\percent}.

\subsection{Mirror fabrication and assembly}
\label{pyramidMOTmirrorfab}
Guided by the diffraction analysis above, we adopt a pyramidal reflector with an aperture $a=\SIrange{0.3}{0.4}{\milli\metre}$, limiting the on-axis residual intensity at the expected MOT position to at most \SI{7}{\percent} of the peak incident intensity whilst preserving the capture volume of the MOT. To realise this, the two adjacent base corners of four gold-coated, right-angle prism mirrors (Thorlabs MRA05-M01) are truncated so that the substrates fit together seamlessly to form a square-based pyramid with a central square aperture at the apex, as was illustrated in \autoref{fig:fingeroverview}. Each prism is secured with a nylon-tipped set screw in a custom aluminium mount (see \autoref{polish}), which holds the mirror at a \SI{45}{\degree} angle during processing.

Material is removed one corner at a time using a fibre-optic polisher (Krelltech SpecPro), which is modified to allow microcontroller-timed operation (Arduino Uno with relay actuation). A typical sequence for the removal of a single corner involves \SIrange{165}{180}{\second} of polishing on \SI{30}{\micro\metre} diamond lapping film, followed by \SI{60}{\second} on \SI{3}{\micro\metre} silicon-carbide film to smooth the facet. Polishing is carried out exclusively towards the gold-coating (see \autoref{polish}) to minimise glass chipping at the edges and the resultant scatter, which can severely limit atom number in a pyramidal MOT.\footnote{Crudely cut prism substrates with rough edges have been shown to limit pyramidal-MOT performance via edge-scattered light~\cite{Arlt1998}. In this study, a MOT capturing over $10^{8}$ atoms within a trapping volume of \SI{1}{\centi\metre\cubed} showed a significant collapse for pyramidal beam intensities in excess of \SI{8}{\milli\watt\per\centi\metre\squared}.} Once the removal of one corner is completed the prism is flipped in the mount to process the adjacent corner.

To determine the polishing time $t_a$ required to reach a target aperture $a$, we adopt a simple material-removal model\footnote{We model only the coarse \SI{30}{\micro\metre} diamond-lapping step, since the subsequent \SI{3}{\micro\metre} finishing pass does not measurably contribute to the removal of material.} based on Preston's law~\cite{Preston1927}. Widely applied in the context of glass lapping and polishing, Preston's law relates the volumetric removal rate $Q$ to the normal load $F$ and the relative speed $v$ between tool and workpiece:
\begin{equation}
Q \coloneqq \frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}t} = k v F\,,
\label{eq:preston-vol}
\end{equation}
where $V$ is the removed volume and $k$ represents the Preston coefficient. Since the mirror mount is free to translate vertically, its weight provides a constant\footnote{The weight of the substrate and its small decrease during polishing does not appreciably affect this load.} normal load for polishing. With the polisher maintaining a fixed relative speed, both $v$ and $F$ are effectively constant during a run. Consequently, the volumetric removal rate is constant and the total removed volume grows linearly with time: $V(t)=Qt$. As illustrated in \autoref{polishtime}, the total removed volume $V$ from each base corner of the substrate---a right-angle prism with edges of equal length $c=\SI{5}{\milli\meter}$---can be expressed in terms of the truncation length $b$ along the edge as $V={b^{3}}/{6}$. The fact that $b = (c-a)/2$, allows us to relate $a$ directly to $c$, $Q$ and $t$ via $V$:
\begin{equation}
V=\frac{(c-a)^3}{48}=Qt
\;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;
a(t)=c-\left( 48Qt \right)^{1/3}\,.
\label{eq:at}
\end{equation}
In order to find the optimal polishing time $t_{\SI{300}{\micro\meter}}$ for an aperture with $a=\SI{300}{\micro\meter}$ via \autoref{eq:at}, we need an accurate estimate of the removal rate $Q$, which we determine empirically from a sequence of trials. A first trial of $t=\SI{180}{\second}$ yields $a=\SI{208}{\micro\metre}$ and thus $Q= \SI{0.01274}{\milli\metre\cubed\per\second}$, which predicts $t_{\SI{300}{\micro\meter}}=\SI{170}{\second}$. A second specimen polished for this time yields $a=\SI{267}{\micro\metre}$. A least-squares fit of \autoref{eq:at} to the two points refines the rate to $Q= \SI{0.01286}{\milli\metre\cubed\per\second}$, implying $t_{\SI{300}{\micro\meter}}=\SI{168}{\second}$. Repeating this procedure, we converge to polishing times that yield four substrates with $a$ in the \SIrange{300}{350}{\micro\metre} range, suitable for assembly into a square-based pyramid. Fitting the model in \autoref{eq:at} to the full data set yields $Q=\SI{0.01280 \pm 0.00007}{\cubic\milli\metre\per\second}$, as illustrated in \autoref{polishtime}.

The mirror surface is protected with a peel-off polymer film (First Contact) and the substrate is secured in a custom brass collet (see \autoref{mirror}) for the milling of two perpendicular trenches that accommodate the right-angle prism mirrors. Small epoxy reservoirs at the edges prevent the adhesive from creeping onto the mirror faces. A central \SI{0.5}{\milli\metre} through-hole provides clearance for the atomic beam emerging from the pyramidal aperture at the apex. During assembly, a custom alignment tool with four PTFE screws enables precise placement of each prism mirror in the holder. Using a top-view microscope with coaxial illumination, the position of each substrate is adjusted until the reflected illumination pattern is continuous across the facet junctions and the aperture edges form a centred square. Once alignment is verified, a small amount of vacuum-compatible, heat-curing epoxy (Epo-Tek 353ND) is dispensed into the reservoirs and the assembly is cured at \SI{120}{\degreeCelsius} for a duration of \SI{15}{\minute}.

\subsection{Cold atom detection}
\label{pyramidMOTcoldatom}


Pyramidal MOTs can be operated either as continuous cold atom sources---maintaining a steady axial scattering force imbalance so that atoms are extracted through the apex at all times---or in a pulsed mode, cycling between loading the MOT and launching the atoms via a push beam. Continuous operation is attractive for its simplicity~\cite{Cacciapuoti2001}, whereas pulsed schemes deliver colder atoms, enabling transfer efficiencies of \SIrange{80}{90}{\percent}~\cite{Swanson1998,Myatt1996}. This is because the quadrupole field is switched off during a brief optical-molasses phase before launch, thereby enhancing sub-Doppler cooling mechanisms. Our aim is to operate the pyramidal MOT in continuous mode for simplicity, avoiding timing sequences and extra control hardware.\footnote{Nothing is lost by this choice: continuous sources can run in a pulsed mode by switching the flux on and off magnetically, optically or mechanically.} A practical requirement for continuous extraction is that the apex opening be smaller than the trapped cloud~\cite{Kim2001}---a criterion that motivated our choice of a sub-millimetre square aperture.

Establishing a stable cold-atom cloud is a prerequisite for operating the pyramidal MOT as a source. To this end, we address the radiation-pressure imbalance caused by the aperture, for which several methods exist~\cite{Arlt1998}. One option is to tilt the mirror assembly so the main cooling beam enters at a slight angle, removing the aperture shadow. Alternatively, an additional cooling beam can block the opening either from the front (entering at an angle) or---more effectively---from the rear, as shown in \autoref{pyramidtest}.

To verify MOT formation under vacuum, we secure the pyramidal-mirror assembly to the stainless-steel mounting structure used for the fibre-tip resonator (\autoref{sec:mounting_structure}). As shown in \autoref{pyramidtest}, a PTFE plate with a circular aperture holds the gold-coated aluminium mirror module while preserving optical access for the cooling beams. The module is mounted in a glass-cell UHV chamber kept at a pressure of $\sim\SI{1e-8}{\milli\bar}$. For the quadrupole field, an anti-Helmholtz pair of coils is aligned with respect to the assembly using a 3D-printed frame on a three-axis translation stage. MOT fluorescence is recorded with a CCD camera placed \SI{30}{\degree} off-axis to avoid the incident cooling-beam path. Because the amount of scattered light from the machined mirror edges is substantial, traces of the MOT are only visible after background subtraction. We therefore record CCD camera frames under continuous illumination with cooling light at zero magnetic field and with a quadrupole field gradient of \SI{11.3}{\gauss\per\centi\meter} (see \autoref{motbfield}). A faint bright region of fluorescence appears near the centre of the pyramidal mirror assembly, suggestive of a magneto-optical trap.

\section{Conclusion}
In this chapter, we have discussed the design, realisation and experimental characterisation of the building blocks that constitute a cavity-compatible platform for single-atoms: a mechanically stable mount for a glass-substrate Fabry---Pérot resonator with optical access for a dipole trap and a compact pyramidal-MOT as a cold atom source.

Finite-element modal response analysis of a legacy fibre-tip resonator mount closely matched both the measured passive-stability spectrum and actuator response function, capturing the dominant fundamental at \SI{267}{\hertz} and a cluster of higher-order modes between \SIrange{1}{7}{\kilo\hertz} that limit the achievable feedback bandwidth for length stabilisation. In light of these findings, we designed a glass-substrate resonator mount that shifts the fundamental mode to \SI{1.89}{\kilo\hertz} and suppresses the associated maximum displacement by nearly two orders of magnitude. The remainder of the spectrum exhibits broad resonance-free intervals that support a higher locking bandwidth.

Guided by prior work and near-field diffraction modelling, we designed and built a compact pyramidal-MOT mirror module with a design aperture of \SIrange{0.3}{0.4}{\milli\meter} that limits on-axis residual light to \SI{7}{\percent} of maximum incident intensity at distances of \SIrange{2}{5}{\milli\meter} from the apex, while preserving MOT capture volume for operation as a continuous cold-atom source. We established a polishing procedure that yields right-angle prism mirrors with well-defined facet truncations and aperture edges reproducibly in the \SIrange{300}{350}{\micro\meter} range. The pyramidal MOT was tested in a stand-alone UHV setup with an axial plug beam and preliminary signatures of a cold atom cloud were observed at the central apex.

Though characterised separately, the mechanical and optical interfaces of the building blocks discussed in this chapter are mutually compatible and permit full integration within a single UHV apparatus. Quantitative flux measurements and direct loading of an intra-cavity dipole trap constitute the remaining steps towards a cavity-enhanced atom–photon interface.
