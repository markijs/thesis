\chapter{Conclusion}
\addtocontents{toc}{\vskip-6pt\par\noindent\protect\textcolor{gray75}{\protect\rule{\textwidth}{0.5pt}}\par}
\label{chap:Conclusion}
The work presented in this thesis was undertaken with the objective of implementing a scalable quantum interface between atoms and photons by combining optical dipole trapping, holographic beam shaping and a Fabry--Pérot resonator. The result is a set of mutually compatible tools which are key to establish such interfaces for quantum networks.

\section{Summary}
The principal quantitative findings that underpin the cavity-based platform central to this work are summarised below.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, labelsep=1em]
\item \emph{Enhanced loading of single-atom dipole traps.}  
By increasing the depth of an optical dipole trap, the transition from fast loading to long-lived trapping was demonstrated. Loading was achieved on a timescale of \SI{2.1}{\second} followed by a measured lifetime of \SI{7.9}{\second}, yielding a single-site filling fraction of \SI{79\pm 2}{\percent}.  This result surpasses the conventional collisional-blockade limit of \SI{50}{\percent}, offering a practical route to defect-free arrays of trapped atoms.

\item \emph{Power efficient holograms for homogeneous trap arrays.}  
A systematic comparison of iterative Fourier transform algorithms demonstrated that weighted Gerchberg--Saxton (GSW) and hybrid methods that combine GSW with the mixed region amplitude freedom (MRAF) algorithm resulted in the best overall performance to realise light patterns suitable for trapping arrays of $4\times4$ atoms. The GSW approach maintained a power efficiency of about \SI{90}{\percent}, a trap intensity coefficient of variation as low as \num{4.2e-7} and the fastest convergence time of \SI{0.8}{\second} among all tested algorithms. These metrics support a reliable computational framework for generating uniform, reconfigurable trap arrays.

\item \emph{Realisation of a high-finesse Fabry--Pérot resonator for strong coupling.}  
The assembled resonator, which consists of a high-transmission near-planar output mirror ($T=\SI{38.2}{ppm}$) and a low-transmission curved micro-mirror ($T=\SI{1.74}{ppm}$), exhibited a finesse of $\num{7.51(9)e4}$ and a linewidth of $\SI{4.20}{\mega\hertz}$. The corresponding parameters $(g_{\mathrm{max}},\gamma,\kappa)=2\pi\times(11.5,3.03,2.10)~\mathrm{MHz}$ yield a single-atom cooperativity $C = 10.4$, firmly placing our resonator in the strong-coupling regime for atom-photon interfacing.

\item \emph{Mechanically stable, cavity-compatible trapping architecture.}  
A resonator mount was developed to provide passive mechanical stability for cavity-length control within a compact ultra-high-vacuum assembly. Finite-element modal analysis confirmed a fundamental resonance at $\SI{1.89}{\kilo\hertz}$ with displacement amplitudes nearly two orders of magnitude smaller than those of previous designs. The resulting structure offers broad resonance-free regions that support high-bandwidth feedback stabilisation and full optical access for high-NA trapping beams.

\item \emph{Integration with a compact pyramidal MOT as a cold-atom source.}  
A four-mirror pyramidal MOT module was fabricated with an apex aperture of $\SI{0.3}{\milli\metre}\times\SI{0.3}{\milli\metre}$. Near-field diffraction modelling showed that reflected, on-axis residual intensity remains below $\SI{7}{\percent}$ of the incident beam within $\SIrange{2}{5}{\milli\metre}$ from the apex, ensuring efficient atomic extraction while preserving the MOT capture volume. Preliminary fluorescence imaging confirmed the formation of a cold-atom cloud at the pyramid centre, demonstrating the module's operational viability.
\end{itemize}
Together, these quantitative results demonstrate the feasibility of combining reconfigurable dipole-trap arrays with a high-finesse resonator in a compact and mechanically robust assembly. Such integrated platform offers a realistic route towards scaling of node capacity and functionality beyond single-emitter implementations in quantum networks.

\section{Outlook}
Building on the designs and experiments presented in this work, the following sections set out the principal near-term and high-impact developments, organised by theme.

\subsection{Towards an integrated light–matter interface}
A natural next step is the full integration of holographically generated dipole-trap arrays with the high-finesse resonator demonstrated here. Deterministic positioning of single atoms within the cavity mode could be realised by dynamic trap steering and feedback-based reloading, building on the occupancy-enhancement schemes developed in \autoref{chap:DynamicDipoleTrapsforSingleAtoms}. This requires the demonstration of atomic transport from the pyramidal MOT to an intra-cavity molasses and subsequent loading into dipole traps.

\subsection{Cavity-mediated protocols for quantum networks}
Once deterministic loading of atoms into cavities is achieved, the platform would be ready for cavity-mediated protocols such as V-STIRAP, enabling high-repetition, deterministic generation of indistinguishable single photons with controllable temporal envelopes. Extending this to a two-node platform would enable direct testing of cavity-mediated entanglement schemes, which is key to establishing proof-of-principle quantum-repeater nodes.

\subsection{Advanced control and stabilisation}
Ongoing experiments aim to implement an active cavity-length stabilisation feedback loop using the glass-substrate resonator mount described in \autoref{chap:CavityCompatibleTrappingArchitectures}. This will allow high-bandwidth suppression of vibrational noise, improving long-term stability for coupling the resonator to atomic transitions. In parallel, automation of holographic trap calibration could be achieved by integrating CCD-based intensity measurements with iterative kinoform optimisation. Such a closed feedback loop would dynamically correct static aberrations and power drift, maintaining uniform trap depths during prolonged experimental runs.

\subsection{Dynamic holography and real-time atom transport}
Dynamic holography offers a powerful route towards the simultaneous and programmable motion of many atoms. By updating the phase patterns on a spatial light modulator in real time, entire arrays of atoms could be repositioned within milliseconds, enabling fast reconfiguration of trap geometries, atom sorting and on-demand coupling to cavity modes. The present bottleneck lies in the computational overhead of generating holograms sufficiently quickly to avoid atom loss~\cite{Lee2017,Kim2019}. In recent work, a convolutional neural network has been used to calculate holograms for real-time rearrangement of large atomic registers, demonstrating that artificial intelligence could overcome this limitation~\cite{Lin2025}.

\subsection{Multi-resonator systems for quantum nodes}
The reconfigurable nature of holographic dipole traps makes the platform well suited to applications beyond single-resonator implementations: arrays of curved micro-mirrors on a shared substrate can form multiple resonators, as demonstrated recently~\cite{Doherty2023}. These could couple to a corresponding array of trapped atoms within a single vacuum chamber, in which dynamic holograms shuttle atoms between storage registers and resonator modes. Such a system could operate as a hybrid quantum processor hub in which local atomic memories are interfaced with photonic channels on demand.

\section{Concluding remarks}
The emergence of a quantum internet---built from scalable nodes and high-fidelity photonic links---will enable secure communication, networked sensing and distributed computation on an unprecedented scale. Beyond these technological applications, the same infrastructure will open new routes to probe the limits of quantum mechanics itself, testing nonlocality, contextuality and possible collapse mechanisms across increasingly macroscopic and gravitational regimes. Collectively, these developments mark the transition from isolated experiments to an interconnected platform---one that will drive transformative technologies and deepen our grasp of nature.
